Day 13: Exercises — Alcohols and redox

Track alcohol and carbonyl transformations, check oxidation states, and connect reagent amounts to the evidence for a product. Use the lecture and demo to revisit a worked case.

Core 1–12: about 80–100 minutes. Transfer 13–20: an optional 45–65 minutes; choose problems that target the mistakes you want to resolve.

Local refresher and worked starting example

Selected primary alcohol oxidation gives aldehyde then acid; secondary alcohol gives ketone. Carbonyl reduction by a supplied suitable reagent gives an alkoxide then an alcohol after proton donation. For the carbon oxidation-state check, C–O bond order contributes+1, C–H−1, C–C0. For amounts use n=m/M and compare n/coefficient.

Worked starting example. Ethanol CH₃CH₂OH→ethanal CH₃CHO raises the changing carbon oxidation state from −1 to+1. In the reverse selected reduction, hydride-equivalent delivery forms CH₃CH₂O⁻ first; water can then donate H⁺ to give ethanol and OH⁻. Each stage has explicit atoms and charge.

Draw structures, identify the relevant atom and explain the reason. A name alone is insufficient where a mechanism or argument is requested.

1. Classify the alcohol centre

Draw CH₃CH₂OH, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ and (CH₃)₃COH. Classify the OH-bearing carbon and count its attached H.

Hint

Count carbon neighbours and H at that particular carbon.

Worked answer and reasoning

The centres are primary with two H, secondary with one H and tertiary with no H. These local differences determine whether the simple carbon-skeleton-retaining carbonyl oxidation pattern applies.

2. Oxidise a primary alcohol once

Draw the selected net transformation ethanol → ethanal, preserving the carbon skeleton. Identify the changing C–O and C–H bond inventory.

Hint

Turn the alcohol-bearing carbon into a carbonyl carbon.

Worked answer and reasoning

CH₃CH₂OH becomes CH₃CHO. The changing carbon loses a C–H bond overall and gains C–O bond order; O also loses its H. This is a net oxidation map, not an elementary one-step mechanism or a lab protocol.

3. Oxidise the aldehyde further

Draw ethanal → ethanoic acid and explain why an aldehyde target requires controlled oxidation conditions.

Hint

Replace the aldehyde C–H with the acid C–OH in the net structure.

Worked answer and reasoning

CH₃CHO becomes CH₃COOH. Since the aldehyde can undergo further oxidation, an unspecified oxidation arrow does not guarantee that isolation stops there. Conditions and reagent scope matter.

4. Oxidise a secondary alcohol

Predict the selected oxidation product of butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃. Name the functional group and give its condensed structure.

Hint

Retain both carbon substituents on the OH-bearing carbon.

Worked answer and reasoning

The product is butan-2-one, CH₃C(=O)CH₂CH₃, a ketone. Forming C=O while removing the appropriate H atoms retains the four-carbon skeleton.

5. Explain the tertiary boundary

Why would drawing (CH₃)₃C=O as the direct simple oxidation product of (CH₃)₃COH be invalid?

Hint

Count bond orders at the central carbon.

Worked answer and reasoning

Three C–C single bonds plus C=O give bond-order sum five. The starting tertiary centre has no C–H for the usual alcohol-to-carbonyl change. Oxidative C–C cleavage can occur under other conditions, but that is a different transformation.

6. Assign a carbon oxidation state

Use +1 per C–O bond order, −1 per C–H and zero per C–C to assign the changing carbon in ethanol, ethanal and ethanoic acid.

Hint

Double C=O contributes plus two.

Worked answer and reasoning

The values are −1, +1 and +3 respectively. Increasing oxidation state identifies oxidation even when simply counting oxygen atoms would miss the first change.

7. Check a ketone carbon

Assign the carbonyl-carbon oxidation state in CH₃COCH₃ and the corresponding OH-carbon value in CH₃CH(OH)CH₃.

Hint

The ketone carbon has no attached H; the alcohol carbon has one.

Worked answer and reasoning

Propanone carbonyl C is +2 from C=O. Propan-2-ol carbinol C is 0 from +1 for C–O and −1 for C–H. The ketone-to-alcohol transformation lowers the carbon oxidation state by two.

8. Reduce an aldehyde

Assume a suitable NaBH₄ reduction then proton-donor work-up of CH₃CH₂CHO. Predict the neutral organic product.

Hint

Carbonyl reduction preserves the carbon skeleton and adds H at carbon.

Worked answer and reasoning

Propanal gives propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH. Hydride-equivalent delivery gives an alkoxide first; a proton donor supplies its O–H proton afterward.

9. Reduce a ketone

Under the same selected reduction/work-up model, predict CH₃COCH₂CH₃ → product. Does the product have a stereocentre?

Hint

The former carbonyl carbon gains H and OH while retaining methyl and ethyl.

Worked answer and reasoning

The product is butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃. Its OH-bearing carbon has OH, H, CH₃ and CH₂CH₃, so it is a stereocentre. An achiral reagent/environment can allow both faces of the planar carbonyl to react.

10. Draw the reduction arrows

For propanone, draw a simplified H:⁻ delivery step and the alkoxide. Explicitly state what H:⁻ represents.

Hint

Move the carbonyl pi pair onto oxygen as C–H forms.

Worked answer and reasoning

The product is (CH₃)₂CHO⁻; one pair forms C–H and the C=O π pair returns to O. H:⁻ means hydride-equivalent delivery from a reagent such as borohydride, not a claim that free hydride persists in aqueous solution.

11. Finish the work-up

Use water to turn (CH₃)₂CHO⁻ into alcohol. Draw the donor and conjugate base instead of writing unexplained H⁺.

Hint

Water loses one H⁺ when it protonates the alkoxide.

Worked answer and reasoning

Alkoxide O attacks water H; the water O–H pair returns to O. Products are propan-2-ol and OH⁻, with net charge −1 conserved.

12. Test reagent scope

A student transfers the selected NaBH₄ aldehyde/ketone rule directly to an ordinary ester and an amide. Why is that unsupported by this course toolkit?

Hint

Carbonyl groups belong to different functional classes with different reactivity.

Worked answer and reasoning

The supplied rule establishes aldehyde/ketone reduction under appropriate conditions. Esters and amides have different electronic and leaving-group chemistry; their behaviour cannot be inferred just from the shared C=O drawing. More reagent/condition information is required.

Note

Optional transfer starts here. Choose problems that address your own gaps; the core route remains1–12.

13. Read two IR observations

A supplied teaching sample loses a broad 3200–3600 cm⁻¹ alcohol feature and gains strong absorption near 1715 cm⁻¹ after a proposed secondary-alcohol oxidation. Explain support and limitation.

Hint

Relate each observation to a functional group, then consider identity.

Worked answer and reasoning

The changes support loss of OH and formation of carbonyl, consistent with the proposed oxidation. They do not uniquely identify the ketone or establish purity; use additional structure and sample evidence.

14. Calculate amounts by hand

A balanced 1:1 aspirin teaching reaction uses 1.3812 g acid at 138.12 g/mol and 1.53135 g anhydride at 102.09 g/mol. Calculate both mmol values and identify the limiting reagent.

Hint

Divide g by g/mol, multiply mol by1000, then compare amount/coefficient.

Worked answer and reasoning

The acid is 0.01000 mol=10.00 mmol and anhydride0.01500 mol=15.00 mmol. The acid limits the 1:1 reaction. Comparing raw grams would ignore the different molar masses.

15. Use a coefficient other than one

For the hypothetical balanced teaching stoichiometry 2 A + B → P, available amounts are 12 mmol A and8 mmol B. Calculate the maximum mmol P and identify the limit.

Hint

Compare n(A)/2 with n(B)/1.

Worked answer and reasoning

A permits 6 mmol reaction extent; B permits 8 mmol. A limits product to 6 mmol P. A larger molar amount can still limit when its stoichiometric coefficient is larger.

16. Calculate theoretical mass

The aspirin teaching reaction can make10.00 mmol product with molar mass180.16 g/mol. Calculate theoretical mass in grams.

Hint

Convert mmol to mol before multiplying by g/mol.

Worked answer and reasoning

0.01000 mol×180.16 g/mol=1.8016 g. Using10 directly as mol would introduce a thousandfold error.

17. Calculate isolated yield

Using theoretical mass1.8016 g and isolated mass1.20 g, calculate isolated yield. State a claim this value cannot establish.

Hint

Yield%=100×isolated/theoretical.

Worked answer and reasoning

The result is about 66.6%. It does not prove purity or conversion: isolation losses, unreacted material, side reactions and retained impurities require separate evidence.

18. Repair a yield over 100 percent claim

A wet isolated sample weighs 1.95 g although the theoretical pure-product maximum is 1.8016 g. Should this establish a 108% chemical yield? Give a better interpretation.

Hint

The balance measures all material present, not only intended product.

Worked answer and reasoning

The apparent mass-based percentage is about 108%, but a pure-product yield above the stoichiometric maximum is inconsistent with the assumptions. Residual solvent/water, impurities or input/measurement errors should be investigated before reporting an actual product yield.

19. Plan a one-step interconversion

Given ethanal and a target of ethanol, choose oxidation or the supplied carbonyl reduction/work-up. Draw the target and explain why further oxidation is wrong.

Hint

The target carbon has more C–H and less C–O bond order.

Worked answer and reasoning

Use the selected reduction followed by proton donation to give CH₃CH₂OH. Oxidation moves toward ethanoic acid instead, increasing rather than decreasing the changing carbon oxidation state.

20. Assemble an evidence statement

A proposed propan-2-ol→propanone experiment gives an IR carbonyl band, one visible TLC spot and an isolated mass. Write two supported conclusions and two unresolved questions.

Hint

Separate functional-group evidence, chromatographic observation and chemical identity.

Worked answer and reasoning

The IR supports a carbonyl-containing sample; the TLC records one spot under its stated conditions. Neither alone proves pure propanone. Identity needs additional structural evidence, and purity/conversion require suitable separate checks; the isolated mass alone is not enough.

R task: chemistry calculation in your report

The balanced teaching equation is salicylic acid + acetic anhydride → aspirin + ethanoic acid, with all coefficients one. The shared CSV supplies reagent masses and molar masses. This is a calculation context, not a synthesis protocol. Keep the raw data unchanged.

The amount in mol is mass in g divided by molar mass in g/mol. Divide each reactant amount by its stoichiometric coefficient. The smallest available reaction extent limits the maximum product amount. Here aspirin’s coefficient is one and its supplied molar mass is 180.16 g/mol.

Predict which of 10.0 and 15.0 mmol will limit a 1:1 reaction, then add this code to your report:

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
amounts <- read_csv("data/amounts.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(amount_mol = mass_g / molar_mass_g_mol,
         amount_mmol = amount_mol * 1000,
         extent_mol = amount_mol / stoich)
knitr::kable(amounts, digits = 4)
limiting_extent_mol <- min(amounts$extent_mol)
theoretical_product_mass_g <- limiting_extent_mol * 180.16
theoretical_product_mass_g

Knit the report. Give the limiting reagent, theoretical product amount in mmol and mass in g. Change a copy of the acetic anhydride input to 0.51045 g and predict the new limiting reagent before recalculating. Do not overwrite the original CSV to make this alternative scenario.

Numerical and chemical check

The supplied amounts are 0.0100 mol acid and 0.0150 mol anhydride. Salicylic acid limits the product to 10.0 mmol, corresponding to 1.8016 g aspirin. With 0.51045 g anhydride at 102.09 g/mol, anhydride is 5.00 mmol and limits product to 5.00 mmol, or 0.9008 g. Comparing masses alone would be wrong because the reagents have different molar masses. In a different balanced reaction, coefficients other than one must also be included.

Supplied model and its limits

The displayed value is the oxidation state of the changing carbon, not its formal charge. C–O bond order contributes +1, C–H −1, C–C 0. The supplied selected net maps are primary alcohol→aldehyde→acid and secondary alcohol→ketone. The tertiary centre has no attached H, so the simple carbon-skeleton-retaining map cannot be used. Other harsh, C–C-cleaving chemistry is outside this model; no laboratory conditions are supplied.
