Day 21: Exercises — Structure from spectra

Use molecular formulas, IR observations and NMR patterns to test proposed structures.

Core exercises 1–10: begin here. Transfer 11–16: apply the same ideas to new cases. Further checks 17–20: use these to investigate mistakes and revise. Allow roughly 2–3 hours across the unit, with more time for complete mechanism drawings. Attempt each problem before opening its hint, then compare your reasoning with the worked explanation.

Working refresher

All numerical spectra below are simulated teaching examples. For ordinary closed-shell C/H/O formulas use DBE=(2C+2−H)/2; O is omitted from this simple count. IR transmittance dips indicate absorption: broad alcohol O–H is often near 3200–3600 cm⁻¹, acid O–H can extend broadly around 2500–3300 cm⁻¹ and carbonyls occupy roughly 1650–1800 cm⁻¹ depending on structure. In our simple first-order proton NMR model, n equivalent adjacent H give n +1 lines; integral gives relative total H area. A singlet/triplet/quartet has 1/3/4 lines. These shortcuts do not describe all real coupling or exchange. Chemical shifts are in ppm; supplied values are illustrative. Candidate tests and drawings are available in the lecture.

1. Calculate one formula constraint

For an ordinary closed-shell C/H/O molecule C₄H₈O₂, calculate DBE=(2C+2−H)/2. State two structural possibilities consistent with that degree without claiming a unique structure.

Hint

O does not enter this simplified expression.

Worked explanation

DBE=1. One double bond, such as C=O, or one saturated ring could supply it. The formula alone does not establish ester, acid or any unique connectivity.

2. Check two proposed formulas

Count the atoms in CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ and CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH. Verify whether both can be U1 with C₄H₈O₂ and name their functional-group difference.

Hint

Write the carbonyl explicitly before counting.

Worked explanation

Each is C₄H₈O₂. Ethyl ethanoate is an ester, while butanoic acid is a carboxylic acid. Both include one C=O, satisfying DBE1, but the oxygen/hydrogen connectivity differs.

3. Reject a formula mismatch

A proposed candidate for C₄H₈O₂ is ethanol CH₃CH₂OH. Count its atoms and explain why a plausible-looking O–H IR band would not rescue it as the whole U1 structure.

Hint

A matched functional group does not replace the molecular formula constraint.

Worked explanation

Ethanol is C₂H₆O, not C₄H₈O₂. Even if it shared an OH feature with some spectrum, it would not account for U1’s total composition. A full candidate must satisfy all supplied constraints.

4. Read a transmittance trace

A simulated IR trace falls from 95% to 30% transmittance near 1740 cm⁻¹. Which position indicates stronger absorption, and why must you inspect the y-axis label?

Hint

Transmittance measures the fraction of incident light passing through.

Worked explanation

30% transmittance means less light transmitted and therefore stronger absorption than 95% at the comparison region. In an absorbance plot stronger absorption would normally be higher; the same curve direction cannot be assumed without reading the axis.

5. Use an acid-region prediction

Candidate A is ethyl ethanoate and B is butanoic acid. The supplied complete simulated IR has C=O but no broad O–H envelope across the usual acid region. Which candidate is challenged, and what experimental limitation could weaken that argument?

Hint

Ask what each candidate predicts in a measured, sufficiently sensitive region.

Worked explanation

Butanoic acid predicts broad acid O–H, so its absence challenges B. Missing spectral coverage, weak signal or obscuration would weaken absence evidence. Ester A fits this functional-group comparison but still needs other evidence.

6. Compare alcohol and carbonyl evidence

A teaching trace has broad O–H near 3300 cm⁻¹ and no evident C=O region band. Compare ethanol and ethanal as candidates, assuming adequate coverage and sensitivity.

Hint

Ethanol contains OH; ethanal contains C=O but no OH.

Worked explanation

The trace supports ethanol over ethanal for this pair. It does not uniquely prove ethanol against all possible alcohols; the carbon skeleton still requires further constraints.

7. Avoid a unique-peak claim

A student assigns a whole molecule solely because its IR has a band near 1740 cm⁻¹. Explain why this is too strong and name two independent kinds of additional information used here.

Hint

Functional-group regions occur in many related molecules.

Worked explanation

A carbonyl-region band constrains a functional group rather than uniquely specifying connectivity. Molecular formula and proton/carbon NMR supply independent information about composition and atom environments.

8. Interpret a quartet and integral

A simulated signal at 4.12 ppm is a quartet integrating to 2H. Under the supplied simple first-order n +1 model, give the number of neighbouring equivalent H and a plausible fragment.

Hint

Line count and integral describe different things.

Worked explanation

Four lines imply three neighbouring equivalent H. The 2H integral supports CH₂ beside CH₃; a downfield O–CH₂–CH₃ environment is consistent with the shift. That fragment still needs to fit the whole formula.

9. Interpret a triplet and integral

A simulated signal at 1.25 ppm is a triplet integrating to 3H. Under the same simple model, state adjacent-H count and how it can pair with a 2H quartet.

Hint

A triplet has n +1=3, so n=2.

Worked explanation

A CH₃ next to CH₂ can give three lines and 3H integration. Paired with a 2H quartet, it supports an ethyl fragment. The triplet alone does not say whether the remote atom is O, C or another group.

10. Explain a singlet structurally

For CH₃C(=O)OCH₂CH₃, explain why the acetyl CH₃ can be a 3H singlet near 2 ppm while the ethyl CH₃ is a triplet. Ignore small long-range couplings.

Hint

Identify the atom directly adjacent to each methyl.

Worked explanation

Acetyl CH₃ is adjacent to carbonyl carbon with no attached H, so no ordinary vicinal splitting is expected. Ethyl CH₃ is adjacent to CH₂ with two H, giving a triplet in this simple model.

11. Normalise integrals

A spectrum reports integral areas 6, 6 and 4 for the three signal groups of a candidate with eight H. Normalise the ratio and explain why quartet line count is not a hydrogen count.

Hint

Divide by a common factor and compare the total.

Worked explanation

The ratio reduces to 3:3:2, totalling eight H. Splitting distributes a signal’s area across lines; a quartet can represent two equivalent H. Its four lines do not mean four H.

12. Avoid using peak heights as areas

One line of a singlet is twice as tall as one line of a triplet. Can you infer relative H counts? State what quantitative information is required.

Hint

A multiplet spreads total area over several lines.

Worked explanation

No. Use integrated area over the full signal group, with a consistent integration scale. Individual line heights are not proton counts, especially when signal shapes or widths differ.

13. Assign all proton environments in U1

U1 is C₄H₈O₂ with 1.25 ppm triplet 3H, 2.05 ppm singlet 3H and 4.12 ppm quartet 2H. Assign every group to CH₃COOCH₂CH₃, explicitly connecting multiplicity and integral.

Hint

Start with the linked CH₂–CH₃ pair, then place the remaining methyl.

Worked explanation

The terminal ethyl CH₃ is 1.25 t 3H; O–CH₂ is 4.12 q 2H. The acetyl CH₃ is 2.05 s 3H, with no H on adjacent carbonyl carbon. The groups account for all eight H and the ester connectivity.

14. Assign four carbon environments

A simulated decoupled ¹³C spectrum for CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ gives 14,21,60 and 171 ppm. Assign them and state why line height is not normally a quantitative carbon count.

Hint

Separate carbonyl, O-bound methylene and the two methyl environments.

Worked explanation

171 is ester C=O, 60 is O–CH₂, 14 terminal ethyl CH₃ and 21 acetyl CH₃. Routine ¹³C intensities depend on measurement/relaxation effects, so do not count atoms from heights.

15. Interpret signal count with symmetry

A candidate has six total carbons but only four ¹³C environments. Is that automatically impossible? Explain using the distinction between atoms and equivalent environments.

Hint

Equivalent carbons can share a resonance.

Worked explanation

No. Symmetry or equivalence can make several carbon atoms share one signal. Four environments do not always mean four total carbons. The formula, structure and measurement conditions determine whether that equivalence is plausible.

16. Reject butanoic acid with two clues

Use U1’s complete simulated IR (C=O, no broad OH) and proton peaks 1.25 t 3H,2.05 s 3H,4.12 q 2H. State two observations conflicting with CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH, rather than citing the sample name.

Hint

An acid lacks O–CH₂ and predicts acid OH.

Worked explanation

The acid predicts broad carboxylic O–H and no O–CH₂ quartet. It also lacks the isolated acetyl methyl singlet arrangement. A terminal methyl triplet could fit both structures, so that feature alone does not reject the acid.

17. Reject a hydroxyketone alternative

Candidate CH₃C(=O)CH(OH)CH₃ also has C₄H₈O₂. Compare its predicted OH-bearing proton count and adjacent methyl multiplicity with U1’s 4.12 q 2H and 1.25 t 3H.

Hint

The oxygen-bearing carbon is CH, not CH₂.

Worked explanation

The hydroxyketone has a one-H OH-bearing CH and a methyl adjacent to one H, commonly giving a doublet. It does not give the supplied two-H O–CH₂ quartet/three-H ethyl triplet pattern. Its OH adds another predicted difference.

18. Distinguish support from proof

A supplied candidate fits formula, IR and simple NMR while two alternatives fail. Write an appropriately restrained identity conclusion and one remaining limitation.

Hint

Specify the compared candidates and the nature of the observations.

Worked explanation

The candidate best fits the supplied evidence among the tested structures. A finite comparison does not exclude every imaginable alternative, and real confidence depends on spectrum quality, assignment completeness and sample context.

19. Check spectral provenance

An IR file is numerically generated for teaching and named ethyl_ethanoate.csv. Which claims can the plot support, and which claims about an actual laboratory isolate would be unjustified?

Hint

Separate a model demonstration from measured sample evidence.

Worked explanation

It can demonstrate a labelled functional-group pattern and plotting workflow. It cannot establish that a real isolate was measured, identified or pure. The filename records the intended teaching label, not independent experimental identity proof.

20. Plan an independent next check

An unknown’s carbonyl IR fits both an ester and another carbonyl candidate. You can request either a molecular formula or repeat the same isolated peak label. Which is more informative here, and why?

Hint

Seek an observation that tests a different prediction.

Worked explanation

The formula adds an independent composition/unsaturation constraint. Repeating the same peak label without new data adds little. A full proton NMR could likewise test distinct connectivity predictions; choose evidence that discriminates the remaining alternatives.

R task: present an IR comparison

Open the student project. The supplied data/ir.csv contains sample_id, wavenumber_cm1 and transmittance_percent. Plot the simulated ethyl ethanoate and ethanoic acid traces. Keep their sample labels so the plotted evidence can be traced back to the data.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
ir <- read_csv("data/ir.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
comparison <- ir |>
  filter(sample_id %in% c("ethyl_ethanoate", "ethanoic_acid"))
ggplot(comparison,
       aes(wavenumber_cm1, transmittance_percent, colour = sample_id)) +
  geom_line() +
  scale_x_reverse() +
  labs(x = "Wavenumber (cm−1)", y = "Transmittance (%)",
       colour = "Simulated sample") +
  theme_minimal()

Add a report caption explaining that these are simulated traces, that dips indicate absorption in a transmittance plot, and which region distinguishes the candidates. Use a descending wavenumber axis; it is conventional here and does not reverse the underlying physics. Check that transmittance values lie between 0 and 100. Do not fit a model or ask R to assign the unknown for you.

Interpretation check

The acid trace includes a very broad O–H region, while the ester trace lacks that broad feature. Both contain carbonyl absorption. The chemical comparison uses the functional groups expected from the candidate structures; the plot is a way to inspect and communicate the supplied observations. An exact carbonyl position is not a unique molecular fingerprint.

Supplied data and worked check

U1 has carbonyl absorption without a broad O–H feature and proton signals 1.25 ppm triplet, 3H; 2.05 ppm singlet, 3H; 4.12 ppm quartet, 2H. Ethyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃, accounts for an ethyl pair and an acetyl methyl singlet. Butanoic acid predicts acid O–H and lacks the O–CH₂/acetyl pattern.

U2 is C₂H₆O, with broad O–H, no carbonyl, and 1.18 ppm triplet, 3H; 3.65 ppm quartet, 2H; an exchangeable broad OH signal, 1H in this illustrative model. Ethanol fits; dimethyl ether has the same formula but no OH and would not give that proton pattern.

Model limits

Peak positions, shapes and exchange depend on conditions. The listed patterns are introductory first-order examples, not a general spectral solver. Green means the candidate fits the selected supplied evidence, not that every possible alternative is excluded or the sample is pure. A filename is not identity evidence.
