Day 8: Exercises — Electron flow and mechanisms

Draw electron-pair movements and audit atoms, bond orders and formal charges at every step. Core 1–12: about 80–100 minutes. Transfer 13–20: an optional 45–65 minutes; choose problems that target your mistakes.

Local refresher and worked starting example

Full-headed curved arrows move electron pairs. Start each tail on a lone pair or bond; end at an atom or new-bond location. Ordinary carbon has total bond order four. A transition state is a free-energy maximum and an intermediate a minimum. All drawings must conserve atoms and net charge.

Worked starting example. For OH⁻+CH₃Br, move an oxygen lone pair to methyl C and the C–Br pair to Br in one step. Products CH₃OH+Br⁻ have the same atoms and net charge −1. This is our worked SN2 electron-accounting pattern.

Draw structures, identify the relevant atom and explain the reason. A name alone is insufficient where a mechanism or argument is requested.

1. Follow a proton transfer

Draw CH₃OH + OH⁻ ⇌ CH₃O⁻ + H₂O, including both curved arrows and every reacting oxygen lone pair. Audit all atoms and net charge.

Hint

Begin at a hydroxide lone pair; also move the methanol O–H bond pair.

Worked answer and reasoning

The hydroxide pair forms its new O–H bond while the methanol O–H pair returns to methanol oxygen. Both sides contain C₁H₅O₂ and total charge −1. This draws a possible proton transfer; its equilibrium position needs compatible acid-strength data.

2. Repair a proton arrow

A student draws an arrow from the H of H₃O⁺ to the oxygen of NH₃. Identify two errors and replace the step with a valid ammonia protonation drawing.

Hint

Which atom does ammonia contain, and which species supplies the electron pair?

Worked answer and reasoning

Ammonia contains nitrogen, not oxygen. The pair starts on nitrogen and points to a hydronium H; the donor O–H pair returns to O. Products are NH₄⁺ and H₂O, preserving charge +1. Arrows follow pairs, not the proton path.

3. Construct a methyl substitution

Draw OH⁻ + CH₃Br → CH₃OH + Br⁻ as one elementary SN2 step. Explain why two arrows do not imply two steps.

Hint

Pair donation to carbon and departure of Br occur together.

Worked answer and reasoning

The hydroxide lone pair forms C–O and the C–Br pair goes to Br in the same concerted event. There is one barrier and no intervening carbocation. Both sides have net charge −1.

4. Draw the reverse bond changes

As a formal electron-accounting exercise, draw Br⁻ attacking the methyl carbon of CH₃OH with OH⁻ departing. Explain why balanced arrows do not establish that this reverse event is experimentally favourable.

Hint

Keep the bond-pair directions valid, then evaluate the leaving group.

Worked answer and reasoning

A Br lone pair points to methyl C and the C–O bond pair points to O, producing CH₃Br and OH⁻. Atoms and charge balance, but hydroxide is a poor leaving group. Electron accounting is necessary; energies and conditions still determine plausibility.

5. Locate the electrophile

Draw CH₃CHO with oxygen lone pairs and partial charges. Mark the usual attacked atom in hydroxide addition and distinguish partial from formal charge.

Hint

Unequal sharing in C=O leaves one atom relatively electron-poor.

Worked answer and reasoning

O is δ− and carbonyl C is δ+. Hydroxide attacks C while the π pair moves to O. The neutral starting Lewis structure has formal charge zero at both atoms; δ+ is not a carbocation formula.

6. Repair five bond orders

A proposed product of OH⁻ addition to CH₃CHO retains C=O and adds C–OH while keeping C–H and C–CH₃. Count carbon bond order, then repair the step.

Hint

The carbonyl π pair cannot remain a bond if an extra sigma bond forms.

Worked answer and reasoning

The proposed carbon has bond-order sum five. Moving the C=O π pair to O gives CH₃CH(OH)O⁻, a four-single-bond carbon and net charge −1. Show both arrows in the addition step.

7. Track hydride-equivalent addition

Represent a hydride equivalent from NaBH₄ as H:⁻ for a simplified step. Add it to CH₃COCH₃; draw both arrows and the immediate product, not the worked-up alcohol.

Hint

The incoming H makes C–H; the original carbonyl oxygen receives the π pair.

Worked answer and reasoning

The product is (CH₃)₂CHO⁻. H-delivery forms C–H while C=O becomes C–O⁻. H:⁻ is a shorthand for delivery from the reagent, not free hydride present in water; protonation is a later step.

8. Supply a missing proton donor

A drawing changes (CH₃)₂CHO⁻ directly to (CH₃)₂CHOH without another species. Complete it using water, including all products and arrows.

Hint

Water must become its conjugate base after losing H⁺.

Worked answer and reasoning

Alkoxide O donates a pair to water H; the water O–H pair returns to water O. Products are propan-2-ol and OH⁻. Net charge remains −1, and no H appears without a source.

9. A neutral nucleophile gains charge

Draw NH₃ + CH₃Br → CH₃NH₃⁺ + Br⁻ as an SN2 electron-flow step. Count nitrogen bonds and lone pairs before and after.

Hint

Neutral ammonia has three bonds and one pair available for the new bond.

Worked answer and reasoning

The N pair forms N–C as the C–Br pair moves to Br. Nitrogen now has four bonds, no lone pair and formal charge +1. Total charge is zero on both sides; neutral methylamine would require a later deprotonation.

10. Base or nucleophile in this event?

Compare OH⁻ attacking CH₃Br at carbon with OH⁻ removing H⁺ from H₃O⁺. Draw each step and name the role in each.

Hint

Classify the particular attacked atom rather than permanently labelling OH⁻.

Worked answer and reasoning

Attack at methyl C forms methanol and bromide: OH⁻ acts as nucleophile. Attack at H of hydronium produces two waters: OH⁻ acts as base. Both use pair donation, but they ask different chemical questions.

11. Heterolysis as bookkeeping

Draw formal C–Br heterolysis for CH₃Br and assign both product charges. Then explain why that picture alone is not a suitable SN1 proposal for methyl bromide.

Hint

Give the entire bond pair to Br, then consider the methyl cation.

Worked answer and reasoning

Formal products are CH₃⁺ and Br⁻. This conserves net charge zero, but the methyl carbocation is extremely poorly stabilised. A charge-balanced drawing does not establish a readily accessible intermediate; methyl SN2 avoids it.

12. Count steps in a profile

Sketch a reaction profile containing two maxima separated by one minimum. Label every maximum and the intervening minimum. How many elementary steps are depicted?

Hint

A step goes from one minimum to the next through a maximum.

Worked answer and reasoning

Two transition states separate reactants, an intermediate and products, so two elementary steps are shown. The number of curved arrows within either step can be greater than one.

Note

Optional transfer starts here. Choose problems that address your own gaps; the core route remains1–12.

13. Choose the higher barrier

Two alternative paths start from the same reactants and have activation free energies 15 and 25 kJ/mol at the same temperature. Which path is kinetically favoured, all else comparable? Is either final product proportion specified?

Hint

Compare barriers for rate; distinguish endpoint energies for equilibrium.

Worked answer and reasoning

The 15 kJ/mol path has the lower activation barrier and is faster under the stated comparison. The barrier values alone do not specify the relative equilibrium amounts of final products, which require their thermodynamic relationship.

14. Calculate a reverse barrier

A one-step schematic has reactants at 0, transition state at +45 and products at −20 kJ/mol. Give forward barrier, reverse barrier and overall ΔG.

Hint

Measure each barrier upward from its own starting minimum.

Worked answer and reasoning

Forward ΔG‡ is 45 kJ/mol; reverse is 45−(−20)=65 kJ/mol; overall ΔG is −20 kJ/mol. A reaction can be thermodynamically favourable yet have a substantial forward barrier.

15. Add a catalytic path

On the preceding profile, add a catalytic path with its highest relevant barrier at +25 kJ/mol while retaining the same endpoints. Explain what changes and what does not.

Hint

A different path need not alter reactant or product state energies.

Worked answer and reasoning

The catalysed path offers a lower barrier and can speed reaction. The endpoint ΔG remains −20 kJ/mol, so catalysis alone does not change the equilibrium constant for the same net reaction and conditions. A real catalytic path may have several steps.

16. Separate equilibrium from time

A sample has not reached equilibrium after ten minutes, but its eventual equilibrium composition is known. Can that composition alone tell you the half-time? Explain using a profile.

Hint

An endpoint energy difference does not specify the height of the route between endpoints.

Worked answer and reasoning

No. Different barriers can connect the same reactant/product free energies. Equilibrium information constrains relative state stabilities; rates need kinetic information such as the relevant activation barriers and rate laws.

17. Audit a missing charge

A student writes OH⁻ + CH₃CHO → CH₃CH(OH)O with all atoms shown but no product charge. Repair the oxygen electron count and explain why assigning a +1 carbon would not solve it.

Hint

The original carbonyl oxygen now has only one bond.

Worked answer and reasoning

The original carbonyl oxygen is O⁻ with three lone pairs, giving CH₃CH(OH)O⁻. Carbon has four single bonds and formal charge zero. Placing +1 on carbon would not follow from the electron movement or preserve net charge −1.

18. Resonance or reaction?

Draw the two equivalent ethanoate contributors, keeping all nuclei fixed. Contrast their relation with CH₃COOH → CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺.

Hint

One comparison moves electrons only; the other removes a nucleus from the species.

Worked answer and reasoning

Ethanoate contributors differ in which C–O is drawn double and which O carries formal negative charge; they describe one delocalised ion. Acid dissociation changes which atoms belong to the acid species. Use a resonance double-headed arrow for contributors and an equilibrium arrow for dissociation.

19. Preserve atom identity

Label methanol oxygen A and hydroxide oxygen B in methanol deprotonation. Which oxygen belongs to methoxide and which to the newly formed water?

Hint

A proton transfers; an oxygen atom does not move from one molecule to the other.

Worked answer and reasoning

A remains the oxygen of CH₃O⁻; B becomes the oxygen of H₂O. Assigning A to water would imply additional bond changes not present in a simple proton transfer.

20. Build a two-step explanation

Using only CH₃CHO, OH⁻ and H₂O, draw base-catalysed formation of CH₃CH(OH)₂. Cross out the catalyst from the net equation and name the intermediate.

Hint

First add hydroxide, then use water to protonate the alkoxide.

Worked answer and reasoning

Addition gives CH₃CH(OH)O⁻; water then protonates that alkoxide and regenerates OH⁻. The net equation is CH₃CHO + H₂O ⇌ CH₃CH(OH)₂. The alkoxide is an intermediate, while hydroxide is regenerated overall.

Model and limits

Full-headed curved arrows represent electron pairs. Deliberately invalid proposals are included for repair and labelled as such; they are not proposed stable products. The carbonyl case isolates its initial elementary addition only, without claiming a final isolated hydrate yield. A valid ordinary tetrahedral carbon has four bond orders.
