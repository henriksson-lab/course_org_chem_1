Day 7: Exercises — Stereochemistry

Draw and compare stereoisomers, assign R/S and E/Z, and explain each priority decision. Review the lecture when you need a drawing convention. Core 1–12: about 60–80 minutes with drawings and checks. Transfer 13–20: an optional 35–50 minutes for consolidation or later revision. Choose a smaller core subset when a practical session also includes R.

Tools and worked start

For simple tetrahedral stereocentres first require four different groups. CIP priority uses directly attached atomic number, then the first shell of difference; count duplicate atoms for multiple bonds in this comparison. With group 4 away,1→2→3 clockwise=R and anticlockwise=S; reverse if 4 points toward. Fischer horizontal bonds point toward, vertical away. E/Z requires two different groups at each C=C end; same-side higher priorities=Z, opposite=E. For a two-enantiomer mixture, ee=|R%−S%|. Worked start:70:30 gives 40 % ee, not 70 % ee.

Draw first, explain the rule you used, and open the check only after an attempt. Ordinary drawings may use a structure editor or paper.

1. Find a candidate centre

Draw CH₃CH(OH)COOH and list all four substituents at CH(OH). Is it a simple tetrahedral stereocentre?

Hint

Compare complete groups, not only their first atoms.

Worked check

It has OH, H, CH₃, COOH: four different groups. It is a tetrahedral stereocentre.

2. Reject a false stereocentre

Compare CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ and explain why a wedge on OH cannot make the central carbon stereogenic.

Hint

Two methyl groups remain identical regardless of drawing style.

Worked check

The central carbon has two identical CH₃ groups, so it is not a simple stereocentre. Wedges describe geometry but do not change group identity.

3. Rank the first attachment atoms

For a centre attached directly to Br, Cl, OH, H, give CIP priority. Do not use total group size.

Hint

Atomic number decides the first shell.

Worked check

Br>Cl>O(in OH)>H. This compares directly attached atoms, not the number of atoms in each substituent.

4. Resolve the carbon tie in lactic acid

Rank OH, COOH, CH₃, H. Explain the duplicate-atom convention for C=O in this comparison.

Hint

OH attaches through O; COOH and CH₃ both attach through C.

Worked check

OH>COOH>CH₃>H. For the carbon tie, COOH compares as O, O, O and methyl as H, H, H. The duplicate entry is bookkeeping, not an extra real O atom.

5. Read a Fischer depth convention

Draw a cross with COOH top, CH₃ bottom, OH left, H right. Which bonds point toward and away from you?

Hint

Fischer is a convention, not an ordinary planar cross.

Worked check

Horizontal OH/H bonds point toward; vertical COOH/CH₃ bonds point away. The central crossing is the stereocentre carbon.

6. Assign the left-OH Fischer

Use the preceding projection and priorities OH1, COOH2, CH₃3, H4. Trace 1→2→3 and assign R/S with justification.

Hint

The lowest-priority group is horizontal and therefore forward.

Worked check

The trace appears clockwise, but H4 points toward you, so reverse the apparent result. This configuration is S.

7. Assign the mirror Fischer

Keep COOH top and CH₃ bottom, but put H left and OH right. Assign R/S and its relation to the preceding structure.

Hint

One interchange at a single stereocentre inverts configuration.

Worked check

The new projection is R. It is the nonsuperimposable mirror/enantiomer of the S-lactic-acid drawing, with unchanged connectivity.

8. Rotate a Fischer by 180°

Rotate the entire S projection in the plane by 180°. State each new label position and whether configuration changes.

Hint

All four labels rotate together.

Worked check

CH₃ becomes top, COOH bottom, H left, OH right. The configuration stays S. A complete valid 180° Fischer rotation preserves configuration.

9. Test a 90° Fischer rotation

Explain why simply rotating a Fischer cross 90° is unsafe as an identity operation.

Hint

It changes which group positions are interpreted as horizontal versus vertical.

Worked check

The implied toward/away pattern changes under the convention, so a 90° Fischer rotation changes the represented configuration. Redraw using a valid 3D transformation instead.

10. Assign Z-but-2-ene

Draw CH₃CH=CHCH₃ with both methyl groups on the same side. Rank the groups at each end and assign E/Z.

Hint

Methyl carbon outranks H at each alkene carbon.

Worked check

The higher-priority groups are together, giving Z-but-2-ene. Rank each end independently rather than treating left-to-right page direction as a descriptor.

11. Assign E-but-2-ene

Draw but-2-ene with methyls on opposite sides. Explain why ordinary single-bond rotation is not the relevant interconversion.

Hint

The arrangement is around a C=C bond.

Worked check

Opposite high-priority methyl groups give E. Interconversion requires changing the double-bond arrangement; it is not a freely rotating anti/gauche relationship.

12. Check whether E/Z is defined

For CH₂=CHCH₃ and (CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃, test the two-different-substituents requirement at both ends.

Hint

One identical pair at either end is enough to fail.

Worked check

Neither qualifies. Propene has H/H at one end; the second molecule has CH₃/CH₃ at one end. No E/Z descriptor applies to those double bonds.

Optional transfer and challenge

These extend the same concepts without assuming later reaction chemistry.

13. Classify a four-pair set

Classify ethanol/ether, anti/gauche butane, R/S-lactic acid, and E/Z-but-2-ene by their structural relationship.

Hint

Ask connectivity, rotation, mirror relation in that order.

Worked check

Respectively: constitutional isomers; conformers; enantiomers; diastereomers. E/Z pairs are stereoisomers that are not mirror-related.

14. Use a two-centre framework carefully

Use CH₃CH(Br)CH(Cl)CH₃ (2-bromo-3-chlorobutane), numbered from the Br end. Compare the supplied configurations (2R,3R)/(2S,3S) and (2R,3R)/(2R,3S). Classify their relationships; no new R/S assignment from a drawing is required.

Hint

Mirror inversion reverses every centre.

Worked check

RR/SS are enantiomers; RR/RS are diastereomers. The symmetry assumption prevents a hidden meso equivalence from invalidating the simple classification.

15. Examine meso-tartaric acid

Draw a Fischer with COOH top/bottom and OH right/H left at both central carbons. Explain why two stereocentres do not force chirality.

Hint

Inspect the equivalence of its upper and lower halves.

Worked check

The whole structure has an internal symmetry relationship and is meso-tartaric acid, achiral despite stereocentres. Overall superimposability matters, not centre count alone.

16. Reject an R, S shortcut

A student calls every molecule with one R and one S centre meso. Give the missing structural condition.

Hint

Descriptors do not establish equivalence of the two ends.

Worked check

The complete molecule must have appropriate symmetry/superimposability with its mirror. An unsymmetrical R, S molecule can be chiral.

17. Calculate 70:30 excess

A pure two-enantiomer composition is 70 % R and 30 % S. Compute ee and explain why it is not 70%.

Hint

Subtract the two populations.

Worked check

ee=|70−30|=40% in favour of R. Major-enantiomer percentage and excess are different quantities; the excess is the imbalance after pairing equal R/S amounts.

18. Recover composition from excess

Assume only R/S components. A sample has 80 % ee in favour of R. Solve for the two percentages.

Hint

Use R+S=100 and R−S=80.

Worked check

R=90% and S=10%. Adding equations gives 2R=180. Calling this 80 % R would give the wrong excess.

19. Separate configuration and rotation

Does an R label establish positive optical rotation or a stronger receptor effect? State what each additional claim requires.

Hint

Configuration is assigned; rotation/effect is measured.

Worked check

No universal mapping connects R/S to(+)/(−) or biological magnitude. Optical rotation needs specified measurements; receptor effects require appropriate experimental evidence in a chiral environment.

20. Distinguish ee from total purity

R:S is 95:5 among the measured enantiomeric components. Compute ee and explain whether solvent contamination is excluded.

Hint

The measured ratio may describe only a subset of all material.

Worked check

The enantiomeric excess is 90 % R. The ratio does not exclude solvent or other molecular impurities; enantiomeric composition and total chemical purity are separate questions.

R task: distinguish percentage from enantiomeric excess

Question: how unbalanced is each supplied two-enantiomer mixture? The simulated table data/enantiomers.csv contains sample identifiers and percentages of R and S. This model assumes R and S are the only components being counted and have no missing composition. First check that R% + S% = 100.

For this model, ee_percent = abs(R_percent - S_percent). Report the major enantiomer too. Predict the ee for E1 (70% R, 30% S) before running the code.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
mixtures <- read_csv("data/enantiomers.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(total_percent = R_percent + S_percent,
         ee_percent = abs(R_percent - S_percent),
         excess = case_when(R_percent > S_percent ~ "R",
                            S_percent > R_percent ~ "S",
                            TRUE ~ "racemic"))
knitr::kable(mixtures, digits = 1)
ggplot(mixtures, aes(sample_id, ee_percent)) +
  geom_col(fill = "#176b91") +
  labs(x = "Sample", y = "Enantiomeric excess (%)") +
  theme_minimal()

Write one sentence about each sample, specifying both composition and ee. If a sample has 80% ee in favour of R, calculate R% and S% by solving R + S = 100 and R − S = 80. Explain why the graph alone does not establish sample purity or optical-rotation sign.

Numerical and chemical check

E1 has 40% ee in favour of R; E2 (50:50) is racemic, 0% ee; E3 (95:5) has 90% ee in favour of R. An 80% R ee corresponds to 90% R and 10% S. Enantiomeric composition does not establish the absence of other chemical contaminants. Without a validated composition measurement and suitable assumptions this is only a calculation on supplied data. R/S alone does not give optical-rotation sign or a biological effect.

Take-away

Write a valid rule for assigning configuration from a Fischer projection, including the lowest-priority-group correction. Why does this tool predict no optical-rotation sign?

Model and limits

Each horizontal Fischer bond points toward the viewer and each vertical bond away. All operations rearrange the same four lactic-acid groups in that convention. A 180° in-plane Fischer rotation preserves configuration; a 90° layout rotation changes the implied configuration. R/S describes geometry, not measured (+)/(−) optical rotation.
