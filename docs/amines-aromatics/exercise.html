Day 18: Exercises — Amines and aromaticity

Use nitrogen lone pairs, conjugate-acid strengths and aromatic electron counts to explain the structures and reactions. Work through 1–10 for core work, 11–16 for transfer and 17–20 for deeper checks. Allow roughly 2–3 hours across the unit. Attempt each problem before using its hint, then compare your revised reasoning with the worked explanation.

Working refresher

A neutral ordinary amine N has three single bonds and a lone pair; ammonium N has four bonds and formal charge +1. Primary/secondary/tertiary amines have one/two/three carbon substituents on N. Proton-transfer arrows go from base pair to H and from donor H–A bond to A. A larger compatible conjugate-acid pKa means a stronger base; K≈10^(product-acid pKa−reactant-acid pKa). For simple aromatic rings first check planar cyclic conjugation, then count 4 n +2 pi electrons. Pyridine has a separate non-pi N pair; pyrrole uses its N pair in the sextet. In dominant-form pH reasoning, protonated forms predominate below a site’s pKa and deprotonated forms above it. See the local lecture for molecular drawings.

1. Classify carbon substitution at N

Draw CH₃NH₂, CH₃NHCH₃ and (CH₃)₃N with lone pairs. Classify them as primary, secondary or tertiary and count N–H bonds.

Hint

Count carbon groups directly bonded to N, not the total molecular carbon count.

Worked explanation

They are primary with two N–H, secondary with one N–H and tertiary with zero N–H. Each neutral ordinary amine has three single bonds at N and one lone pair.

2. Separate tertiary from quaternary

Compare (CH₃)₃N, (CH₃)₃NH⁺ and (CH₃)₄N⁺. Give carbon-substituent count, N–H count and formal charge for each.

Hint

A protonated tertiary amine still has three carbon groups; quaternary means four carbon groups.

Worked explanation

The three species have respectively 3/0/0, 3/1/+1 and 4/0/+1 for carbon groups/N–H/charge. Only the neutral first species has an available ordinary N lone pair.

3. Draw protonation with a donor

Draw CH₃NH₂ + H₃O⁺ → products using both curved arrows. Count the N bond number before and after and audit net charge.

Hint

The N pair attacks H; the donor O–H pair returns to oxygen.

Worked explanation

Products are CH₃NH₃⁺ and H₂O. N changes from three bonds plus a pair to four bonds/+1. Net charge +1 is conserved; a positive proton is transferred rather than an electron being removed from N.

4. Draw the reverse proton transfer

For CH₃NH₃⁺ + OH⁻, draw deprotonation with both arrows and identify the conjugate acid/base pairs.

Hint

Hydroxide is the pair donor and the ammonium N–H bond is the pair returned to N.

Worked explanation

CH₃NH₂ + H₂O form. The pairs are methylammonium/methylamine and water/hydroxide. Net charge zero on each side; the newly neutral N regains its lone pair.

5. Compare supplied conjugate-acid pKa values

Ethylammonium has approximate aqueous pKa 10.7 and anilinium 4.6. Rank the corresponding neutral bases and state what larger conjugate-acid pKa means.

Hint

A weaker conjugate acid has a stronger conjugate base under comparable conditions.

Worked explanation

Ethylamine is stronger than aniline. Ethylammonium loses H⁺ less readily, so its neutral base binds a proton more favourably in the corresponding comparison. These values do not alone determine all nucleophilic reaction rates.

6. Estimate a nitrogen-to-nitrogen proton transfer

For PhNH₃⁺ + EtNH₂ ⇌ PhNH₂ + EtNH₃⁺, use pKa 4.6 and 10.7 for the acids. Estimate K and identify the favoured side. Ph means phenyl; Et means ethyl.

Hint

Use 10^(product-acid pKa − reactant-acid pKa).

Worked explanation

K≈10^6.1≈1.26×10⁶ toward aniline and ethylammonium. Approximate pKa values must be compatible in solvent and conventions. This equilibrium estimate is not a rate prediction.

7. Distinguish amine from amide

Draw CH₃CH₂NH₂ and CH₃C(=O)NH₂. Identify the N directly connected to carbonyl and explain why its pair differs in availability.

Hint

Only one pair can overlap directly with the neighbouring C=O system.

Worked explanation

The second is an amide. Its N pair delocalises into the carbonyl, contributing partial C–N double-bond character. The first is a simple amine without this direct carbonyl conjugation; treating both as equally basic would ignore structure.

8. Audit an amide contributor

Write CH₃C(O⁻)=NH₂⁺, count N bond orders and sum formal charges. Explain why this drawing is not an independently equilibrating isomer of neutral ethanamide.

Hint

N has a C=N double bond and two N–H single bonds.

Worked explanation

N has four bond orders and +1; O is −1, giving net zero. The neutral and charge-separated drawings are resonance contributors to one molecule with fixed nuclei, not two species undergoing a chemical equilibrium.

9. Explain aniline conjugation

Compare PhNH₂ with PhCH₂NH₂, where Ph is a benzene ring. Which N pair can overlap directly with the aromatic pi system, and what structural interruption appears in the other?

Hint

The intervening CH₂ is an ordinary saturated carbon.

Worked explanation

Aniline PhNH₂ has N directly attached to the aromatic system. In PhCH₂NH₂, the sp³ CH₂ interrupts that direct conjugation. Other substituent and solvation effects can remain; the structural comparison does not claim that only one effect ever controls basicity.

10. Check aromatic requirements

For benzene and cyclohexane, state whether a continuous cyclic pi system exists. For benzene count pi electrons and find n in 4 n + 2.

Hint

A ring of sigma bonds is not sufficient.

Worked explanation

Benzene has a planar continuous pi system and six electrons, giving n=1. Cyclohexane lacks continuous pi conjugation and is nonaromatic, not automatically antiaromatic.

11. Avoid double-counting pyridine electrons

In pyridine, N supplies one electron to the ring pi system and has a separate lone pair outside it. Should the pair be included when counting the aromatic sextet? Predict the effect of protonating that pair.

Hint

Separate the electron in the p orbital from the two in the non-pi lone-pair orbital.

Worked explanation

Do not add the separate pair to the aromatic count. Its protonation makes pyridinium, and the sextet can remain. N becomes positive; the ring skeleton and pi electron count remain consistent with aromaticity.

12. Count pyrrole electrons

A five-membered pyrrole ring has two C=C units and an NH whose pair joins the cyclic pi system. Count the contributions and explain why the N pair is less available than pyridine’s separate pair.

Hint

Each double bond contributes two pi electrons.

Worked explanation

Two C=C units supply four electrons, and N contributes its pair of two, giving six. Removing that pair from its aromatic role by ordinary N protonation costs stabilisation, so pyrrole N is much less basic than pyridine N in this comparison.

13. Repair an aromaticity claim

A student says “Both pyridine and pyrrole contain one nitrogen, so they must have equal basicity.” Give a structural correction using the two lone-pair roles.

Hint

Atom count does not locate the pair’s orbital role.

Worked explanation

Pyridine keeps a non-pi pair available for N–H bond formation; pyrrole’s pair supplies part of the aromatic sextet. Their protonation equilibria differ because the pair environments differ, despite both molecules containing N.

14. Draw glycine at low pH

Use supplied pKa 2.3 for COOH and 9.6 for NH₃⁺. Draw the predominant glycine form at pH 1 and give every formal charge and net charge.

Hint

At pH well below a pKa, the site’s protonated form predominates.

Worked explanation

H₃N⁺CH₂COOH predominates: N is +1, carboxyl group neutral, net +1. Dominant-form drawings are approximations; minority forms still exist.

15. Draw glycine between its pKa values

Using the same supplied pKa values 2.3 and 9.6, draw predominant glycine at pH 6. Explain “zwitterion” and whether net zero implies no formal charges.

Hint

Apply the pH comparison separately to COOH and ammonium.

Worked explanation

H₃N⁺CH₂COO⁻ predominates. COOH has mostly lost H while ammonium mostly retains it. Opposing formal charges sum to zero, so it is a zwitterion; it is not entirely uncharged at every site.

16. Draw glycine at high pH

With pKa values 2.3 and 9.6, draw the predominant form at pH 12. Show the proton transfer from the pH 6 form to hydroxide and audit charge.

Hint

At sufficiently high pH, ammonium can lose a proton while carboxylate remains.

Worked explanation

H₂NCH₂COO⁻ predominates. Zwitterion + OH⁻ → H₂NCH₂COO⁻ + H₂O. N returns to neutral three-bonded form; total charge is −1 on both sides.

17. Calculate a simple amine protonation ratio

For B + H⁺ ⇌ BH⁺, use pKa(BH⁺)=10.0 and [B]/[BH⁺]=10^(pH−pKa). At pH 9 and 11 calculate the ratios and fraction present as BH⁺.

Hint

Convert ratio r=B/BH⁺ to protonated fraction 1/(1+r).

Worked explanation

At pH9, r=0.1 and protonated fraction≈0.909. At pH11, r=10 and fraction≈0.0909. A higher pH reduces the protonated fraction; a ratio is not itself a population fraction.

18. Explain coexistence near pKa

For the same simple amine with pKa(BH⁺)=10.0, predict fractions at pH10. Does every molecule carry half a positive charge?

Hint

At equal pH and pKa, the concentration ratio is one.

Worked explanation

The populations of B and BH⁺ are equal under the model, each about 50%. Individual molecules are in one protonation state or the other; the population average does not mean each N is half protonated.

19. Separate equilibrium from kinetics

Two species have different conjugate-acid pKa values. A student infers which attacks every carbon electrophile fastest. State what the pKa comparison supports and name two missing factors for a rate prediction.

Hint

Basicity compares proton-transfer equilibrium; nucleophilicity is a kinetic property.

Worked explanation

The data compare basicity under compatible conditions. Substrate steric/electronic structure, solvent, leaving group and the particular nucleophile’s solvation can change rates. pKa alone cannot supply a universal nucleophilic-rate ranking.

20. Integrate a peptide-like fragment

For H₂NCH₂C(=O)NHCH₃, identify the simple amine-like N and the amide N. Explain which structural feature you would inspect first before predicting protonation and why the two sites should not be treated identically.

Hint

Follow each N’s directly attached atom: is it a carbonyl carbon?

Worked explanation

The left H₂N site is not directly attached to C=O; the right NH is the amide N. Carbonyl conjugation delocalises the amide pair and changes its basicity. The molecule can contain more than one chemically distinct nitrogen environment.

Consolidate your work

Choose one corrected error and explain what changed in your reasoning. Then complete the self-check. Reading is optional; all practice above is self-contained.

Explore

Question: why can the same pH leave one nitrogen base mainly neutral and another mainly protonated?

Use this activity for about 8 minutes: predict for 2 minutes, explore for 4, then explain your result for 2.

Enable JavaScript to compare the supplied molecular species and aqueous ionisation model.

Predict

The supplied approximate aqueous conjugate-acid pKa values are methylammonium 10.6, anilinium 4.6, and pyridinium 5.2. For a base B and its conjugate acid BH⁺,

\[f_{BH^+}=\frac{1}{1+10^{pH-pK_a}}.\]

Before moving a control, predict the fraction BH⁺ at pH = pKa and at one pH unit below it. Predict which base is most protonated at pH 7.

Explore

  1. Use At supplied pKa for each molecule. Explain the common midpoint.
  2. Compare all three molecules at pH 7 while keeping the environmental pH fixed.
  3. For one molecule, move pH two units upward. Follow both the curve and the drawn neutral/protonated molecular forms.
  4. Use Reset to reconstruct the original state.

Explain and check

At pH = pKa, half the population is BH⁺. One unit below gives \(1/(1+0.1)=10/11\), about 90.9%. At pH 7, methylamine is much more protonated than aniline or pyridine under this model. Higher pH decreases BH⁺, because deprotonated B becomes favoured.

A 50% fraction describes two populations of molecular species; it does not mean that every nitrogen has half a proton. The ring drawings use implicit carbon/hydrogen conventions; the N–H count and charge are shown explicitly.

Model limits

These are supplied, approximate compatible aqueous pKa values in a dilute one-site model. Substituents, solvent, temperature and activities can alter behaviour. The plot predicts neither extraction recovery nor the rate of a nucleophilic reaction. See the local nitrogen exercises for lone-pair and charge explanations.
