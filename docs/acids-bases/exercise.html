Day 3: Exercises — Acids, bases and ionisation

Draw the proton transfers, compare conjugate bases and test the supplied pKa relationships. Use the lecture and the refresher below when needed.

Core 1–12: about 60–80 minutes with drawings and checks. Transfer 13–20: an optional 35–50 minutes for consolidation or later revision. Choose a smaller core subset when the session also includes R.

Tools and worked start

A Brønsted acid donates H⁺ and a base accepts it. Curved arrows start at electron pairs. A conjugate base has one fewer H and charge one lower. Lower compatible pKa means stronger acid. Use K≈101. For the supplied monoprotic dilute aqueous model, r=[A⁻]/[HA]=10^(pH−pKa) and fraction A⁻=r/(1+r). Worked start: at pH=pKa, r=1 and fraction=0.5. All numerical pKa values below are supplied compatible aqueous teaching values.

Draw first, explain the rule you used, and open the check only after an attempt. Ordinary drawings may use a structure editor or paper.

1. Draw both arrows

Draw CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O with relevant lone pairs and both curved arrows.

Hint

One pair goes to H; the original O–H pair stays on acid O.

Worked check

Hydroxide pair→acidic H, acid O–H bond→acid O. Net charge is−1 before and after. The proton moves while its original bonding pair stays behind.

2. Name both conjugate pairs

For the preceding proton transfer, identify acid, base, conjugate acid and conjugate base.

Hint

Pair species differing by one H⁺.

Worked check

CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₂O/OH⁻ are the pairs. Reactants are acid and base; water is the base’s conjugate acid, ethanoate the acid’s conjugate base.

3. Protonate methylamine

Draw CH₃NH₂ + H–A → CH₃NH₃⁺ + A⁻ with two arrows and all N lone pairs/charges.

Hint

N starts with three bonds and a pair.

Worked check

N pair→H; H–A bond→A. N ends with four bonds/no lone pair/charge+1. Total charge is conserved because A becomes−1.

4. Remove H⁺ from ammonium

Draw NH₄⁺ losing one H⁺ and identify the formula, charge and lone-pair count of the conjugate base.

Hint

Keep the N–H bonding pair on N.

Worked check

The conjugate base is neutral NH₃ with three N–H bonds and one lone pair. Losing H⁺ decreases charge by one without removing the bonding electrons.

5. Compare conjugate-base stabilisation

Draw ethoxide and both equivalent ethanoate contributors. Use the supplied aqueous pKa 16 for ethanol and 4.8 for ethanoic acid to identify the stronger acid.

Hint

Explain the base structure before citing the numbers.

Worked check

Ethanoate distributes negative charge over two equivalent O atoms; ethoxide lacks that equivalent delocalisation. Ethanoic acid is stronger, consistent with its lower pKa.

6. Reject a false resonance contributor

A proposed contributor moves a hydrogen from one O to another. Explain why this is not merely resonance.

Hint

Resonance changes electron placement while preserving nuclei.

Worked check

Moving H changes a nuclear position and bonding pattern, describing a proton transfer or tautomeric change. It is not another contributor to the same fixed-nucleus species.

7. Estimate forward equilibrium

For CH₃COOH + CH₃NH₂ ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + CH₃NH₃⁺, use acid pKa 4.8 and product-acid pKa 10.6. Apply K≈10^(product−reactant acid pKa).

Hint

Subtract in the stated order.

Worked check

K≈10^5.8≈6.3×10⁵. Products contain the weaker acid and are strongly favoured in the compatible aqueous approximation. This does not give a reaction rate.

8. Reverse the equilibrium

Write the reverse of the preceding equation and estimate its K.

Hint

A reversed equation has reciprocal K.

Worked check

CH₃COO⁻+CH₃NH₃⁺⇌CH₃COOH+CH₃NH₂ has K≈1/(6.3×10⁵)≈1.6×10⁻⁶. Using the same large K for both directions is inconsistent.

9. Control an inductive comparison

Supplied aqueous pKa values are 4.8 for CH₃COOH and 2.9 for ClCH₂COOH. Draw the conjugate bases and explain the trend.

Hint

The changed substituent is electron withdrawing.

Worked check

Cl substitution stabilises the nearby carboxylate through inductive electron withdrawal, making the acid stronger. Compare within compatible conditions; the Cl is not a resonance contributor moving between atoms.

10. Convert ratio into fraction

At pH 3.8 for a monoprotic acid of pKa 4.8, use r=10^(pH−pKa) and fraction=r/(1+r).

Hint

The fraction denominator includes both forms.

Worked check

r=0.1; fraction=0.1/1.1≈0.0909=9.09%. A ratio of 0.1 does not equal exactly 10% of the total population.

11. Explain the midpoint

At pH=pKa, calculate r and the ionised fraction. Describe definite molecular species rather than half-protonated molecules.

Hint

10^0=1.

Worked check

r=1 and fraction=0.5. Equal HA and A⁻ concentrations coexist; each species is HA or A⁻, not an individual molecule halfway losing a proton.

12. Go one unit above pKa

For pKa 4.8 at pH 5.8, calculate r and fraction. Check direction against the previous two cases.

Hint

The ratio multiplies by ten for each pH unit.

Worked check

r=10 and fraction=10/11≈0.9091. The conjugate-base fraction increases as pH increases, consistent with deprotonation being favoured.

Optional transfer and challenge

These extend the same concepts without assuming later reaction chemistry.

13. Go two units above pKa

For the same acid at pH 6.8, calculate fraction and explain why it is not exactly 100%.

Hint

Use r=100, then include HA in the denominator.

Worked check

fraction=100/101≈0.9901. The model retains a small HA population; a strongly favoured form need not be exclusive.

14. Compare two acids at one pH

At pH 4.8 compare supplied pKa 4.8 and 6.0. Use fraction=1/(1+10^(pKa−pH)).

Hint

The weaker acid has the higher pKa.

Worked check

Fractions are 0.5 and approximately 0.0594. At the same pH the lower-pKa acid is more deprotonated. This comparison uses the stated monoprotic dilute aqueous model.

15. Repair a reversed curve

A student’s fraction_A_minus decreases as pH increases after coding 1/(1+10^(pH−pKa)). Explain the sign error.

Hint

Evaluate the code far above pKa.

Worked check

The exponent is reversed for the A⁻ fraction. Use pKa−pH in this denominator. The student’s expression instead gives the complementary HA fraction under the model.

16. Separate equilibrium from kinetics

A proton-transfer estimate has K=10⁶. Does that number alone give seconds needed to reach equilibrium?

Hint

Compare state preference with pathway barrier.

Worked check

No. K describes equilibrium proportions/activities under the model, not the time scale. Reaction kinetics require additional mechanistic and rate information.

17. Use water in both roles

Write one proton-transfer example with water accepting H⁺ and another with water donating H⁺. Identify the resulting conjugates.

Hint

Use H–A and a sufficiently basic B⁻ as generic partners.

Worked check

H–A+H₂O⇌A⁻+H₃O⁺ shows water accepting. B⁻+H₂O⇌BH+OH⁻ shows water donating. Relative extents depend on compatible acid/base strengths.

18. Check total charge in a mechanism

A drawing turns neutral HA and neutral B into A⁻ and neutral BH. Find the missing formal charge.

Hint

The reactant net charge is zero.

Worked check

BH must be BH⁺ when B accepts H⁺ in this generic transfer. A⁻ plus BH⁺ preserves net zero. Drawing neutral BH would lose a unit of total charge.

19. Distinguish speciation and titration

A graph plots fraction A⁻ against a supplied pH grid. Explain why its x-axis cannot simply be relabelled mL of added base.

Hint

pH is an input here; titrant volume would require a mass-balance model.

Worked check

A titration relation must connect added amount, total volume and equilibria to pH. The speciation curve computes fraction at specified pH and does not supply that connection.

20. Set an extraction boundary

At high aqueous pH an acid is mostly A⁻. List missing information needed to predict mass in an organic phase.

Hint

Speciation is only one part of a two-phase calculation.

Worked check

Partition coefficients for relevant species, phase volumes, starting amount and losses are needed. Aqueous fraction alone does not determine organic recovery or isolated yield.

R task: how much of the acid is ionised?

Question: for a monoprotic acid with supplied pKa 4.8, how does its aqueous ionised fraction change between pH 1 and 9? This is a calculated teaching model, not measured acid dissociation data. Use the dilute-solution approximation.

The ratio is \(r=[A^-]/[HA]=10^{pH-pK_a}\), so the fraction is \(r/(1+r)=1/(1+10^{pK_a-pH})\). pH, pKa and the fraction are dimensionless. At pH = pKa predict 0.5. At one pH unit above pKa predict 10/11.

Add the following to your report, below a sentence stating your prediction:

library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
ionisation <- data.frame(pH = seq(1, 9, by = 0.1)) |>
  mutate(fraction_A_minus = 1 / (1 + 10^(4.8 - pH)))
ggplot(ionisation, aes(pH, fraction_A_minus)) +
  geom_line(colour = "#176b91", linewidth = 1) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 4.8, linetype = "dashed") +
  labs(x = "pH", y = "Fraction present as A−") +
  theme_minimal()
1 / (1 + 10^(4.8 - c(3.8, 4.8, 5.8)))

Knit the report. Explain why the curve rises, why its midpoint is 4.8, and why a fraction of 0.909 does not mean each molecule is 90.9% deprotonated. Change pKa to 6.0, predict the shift and then compare the curves. State why the plot alone cannot predict extraction recovery into an organic solvent.

Numerical and chemical check

At pH 3.8, 4.8 and 5.8 the fractions are approximately 0.0909, 0.5 and 0.9091. A larger pKa shifts the midpoint to higher pH; this weaker acid needs higher pH to achieve the same fraction. Fractions describe a population of molecular species. Extraction needs partition coefficients and phase volumes as well as aqueous ionisation. The supplied curve is not a titration curve: pH is the input, not the calculated consequence of adding a specified titrant volume.

Model and limits
