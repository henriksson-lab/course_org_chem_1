Day 19: Exercises — Carbonyl and acyl chemistry

Compare carbonyl addition and acyl substitution by drawing mechanisms and tracking atoms, charge and amounts. Allow 150 minutes: 10 minutes to recall the key steps, 48 minutes for problems 1–6, 48 minutes for 7–10, 34 minutes for 11–12, and 10 minutes to review. These timings include drawing and comparison; continue unfinished problems later rather than rushing a mechanism.

Local explanations: Acyl chemistry and carbonyl addition.

Working definitions

Use full-headed arrows for electron pairs: start at a lone pair or bond and end at its acceptor. Keep ordinary carbon to four bond orders and conserve atoms and net charge. For compatible proton-transfer data, K≈10^(product-acid pKa−reactant-acid pKa). For amounts use n=m/M and 1 mol=1000 mmol; compare n/coefficient before predicting theoretical product. Apparent isolated yield=100×isolated/theoretical product mass. TLC uses Rf=spot/front distances from the same origin. For simple first-order NMR, n adjacent equivalent H give n +1 lines while integration counts H area.

1. Classify the reactive motif

Draw CH₃CH₂C(=O)OCH₃ and CH₃CH₂CH₂OCH₃. Identify ester versus ether and circle the carbonyl carbon where present.

Hint

Look for C=O directly next to single-bond O.

Worked explanation

The first is methyl propanoate, an ester with acyl carbon. The second is an ether without carbonyl; an oxygen atom alone does not supply an acyl-addition site.

2. Predict hydroxide addition

Draw OH⁻ addition to CH₃CH₂C(=O)OCH₃ with both arrows and all intermediate charges.

Hint

Move the C=O pi pair to O while forming the new bond.

Worked explanation

CH₃CH₂C(O⁻)(OH)(OCH₃) forms. Carbon has four single bonds; incoming OH is neutral and original carbonyl O is negative. Net charge remains −1.

3. Collapse with atom labels

Label the ester carbonyl O as A and methoxy O as B. On collapse of the hydroxide-addition intermediate, which labelled oxygen remains carbonyl and which leaves attached to methyl?

Hint

Follow the original atoms rather than redrawing unlabelled products.

Worked explanation

O-A reforms the carbonyl; O-B leaves as CH₃O-B⁻. The immediate acid is CH₃CH₂C(=O-A)OH. B remains attached to methyl; that C–O bond is not the bond broken.

4. Complete proton transfer

The collapse gives CH₃CH₂COOH and CH₃O⁻. Use supplied pKa 4.9 for the acid and 15.5 for methanol to predict the favoured pair and estimate K.

Hint

Use 10^(product-acid pKa−reactant-acid pKa).

Worked explanation

Propanoate and methanol are favoured. K≈10^(15.5−4.9)≈4×10¹⁰. The basic mixture should not be represented as neutral acid coexisting predominantly with methoxide.

5. Write the net equation

Write complete net base-promoted hydrolysis of CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ by OH⁻, then a separate hydronium work-up equation.

Hint

Hydrolysis consumes hydroxide overall; work-up protonates the carboxylate.

Worked explanation

CH₃CH₂COOCH₃+OH⁻→CH₃CH₂COO⁻+CH₃OH. Then CH₃CH₂COO⁻+H₃O⁺→CH₃CH₂COOH+H₂O. The protonation change is a separate chemical event.

6. Distinguish addition

For CH₃CH₂CHO, draw hydroxide addition and proton transfer from water. Does the carbon-bound H ordinarily leave as H⁻ by the ester analogy?

Hint

An aldehyde lacks the methoxy leaving group present in the ester.

Worked explanation

Addition gives CH₃CH₂CH(OH)O⁻ and water protonation gives CH₃CH₂CH(OH)₂ with OH⁻ regenerated. H⁻ loss is not the normal pathway here; a common first step does not imply identical later chemistry.

7. Audit five bonds

A student writes CH₃CH₂C(=O)(OH)OCH₃ as the tetrahedral intermediate. Count carbon bond orders, repair it and place the missing negative charge.

Hint

Two bond orders belong to C=O.

Worked explanation

The proposed carbon has five bond orders. Opening C=O to C–O⁻ gives four single bonds and retains net −1. Correct electron flow and correct valence are linked checks.

8. Activate an acid carbonyl

Draw protonation of CH₃CH₂C(=O)OH by hydronium. Give the oxygen charge and the water product, then identify the atom ethanol attacks next.

Hint

Carbonyl O accepts a proton; carbon becomes a better electron-pair acceptor.

Worked explanation

CH₃CH₂C(=OH⁺)OH+H₂O forms. Ethanol subsequently attacks acyl carbon, moving the carbonyl pi pair to oxygen; it does not need to exist as ethoxide in the acidic pathway.

9. Audit neutral-alcohol attack

After ethanol attacks that activated carbonyl, write the tetrahedral species and locate the +1 charge. What transfer makes the incoming oxygen neutral?

Hint

The incoming alcohol oxygen now has three single bonds.

Worked explanation

CH₃CH₂C(OH)(OH)(O⁺HEt) has positive charge on incoming O. Water can remove its H, giving neutral OEt and hydronium. Each oxygen charge follows its bond count.

10. Make water the leaving group

From CH₃CH₂C(OH)(OH)(OEt), explain the activation and elimination needed to form ester rather than simply drawing OH⁻ loss.

Hint

Protonate an OH before elimination under the representative acidic conditions.

Worked explanation

An OH₂⁺ substituent can leave as H₂O as the other O pair reforms C=O. The initially protonated ester is then deprotonated. These steps conserve charge and regenerate acid catalyst.

11. Compare amide and ester

Draw CH₃CH₂CONH₂ and its charge-separated contributor. Explain one rotational and one reactivity consequence compared with the ester.

Hint

Use N pair donation and C=O pi-pair movement.

Worked explanation

Contributor CH₃CH₂C(O⁻)=NH₂⁺ gives partial C–N double-bond character, restricting rotation. It also stabilises the amide and reduces N-pair availability; comparable amide acyl substitution is generally less facile than ester substitution under ordinary analogous conditions.

12. Critique a synthesis claim

A student mixes propanoic acid and methylamine in water and labels the immediate product an amide. Supplied acid pKa is 4.9; methylammonium pKa 10.6. Predict the initial acid–base outcome and explain the missing synthetic requirement.

Hint

The product acid in proton transfer is the ammonium ion.

Worked explanation

Propanoate+methylammonium are favoured, K≈10^5.7. Proton transfer is not amide formation. A productive coupling requires suitable activation or conditions; a salt drawing does not establish a new acyl C–N bond.

Review your reasoning

Choose two corrected errors: one in a drawing/calculation and one in an interpretation. Explain why the corrected answer follows from the supplied inputs. Keep the first attempt so you can see the change in your reasoning.

Question: can you predict a favoured proton-transfer direction from the reactant and product acids, without confusing equilibrium with reaction speed?

Allow 8 minutes: predict for 2, compare cases for 4, and explain for 2. All numerical inputs and reacting species are supplied in the workbench.

Predict

For the reaction HA + B ⇌ A + BH, compare the conjugate-pair pKa values using

\[K\approx10^{pK_a(BH)-pK_a(HA)}.\]

Charges depend on the chosen pair; the actual charged formulas are displayed. The supplied approximate aqueous acid pKa values are ethanoic acid 4.8, methylammonium 10.6, phenol 10.0 and pyridinium 5.2. A lower pKa means a stronger acid within this compatible comparison.

Predict the result of ethanoic acid with methylamine before using the controls.

Explore

  1. Inspect all four species, not only the numerical constant.
  2. Try Equal pair. Why does swapping a proton within the same conjugate-pair bookkeeping give K = 1?
  3. Try Reactant-favoured case and identify the weaker acid on each side.
  4. Make your own product-favoured comparison and check the net charge.

Explain and check

Ethanoic acid plus methylamine gives a pKa difference of \(10.6-4.8=5.8\), so \(K\approx6.3\times10^5\) toward ethanoate plus methylammonium. Phenol plus pyridine gives \(5.2-10.0=-4.8\), so \(K\approx1.6\times10^{-5}\) and reactants are favoured. Neither value specifies how rapidly equilibrium is approached.

Model limits

The values are approximate and used under a common aqueous comparison. Standard-state, activity, solvent and temperature conventions must be compatible when transferring this calculation to other datasets. K alone also does not give the final composition without starting amounts and an appropriate equilibrium model. No equilibrium solver or laboratory protocol is implied.
