Day 24: Exercises — Spectral evidence and synthesis calculations

Compare the spectral evidence, test a proposed structure and check the associated synthesis calculations.

Allow 150 minutes: 10 minutes for retrieval, 48 for exercises 1–6, 48 for 7–10, 34 for 11–12 and 10 to review your solutions. Drawing and comparison are included in that time. Finish remaining problems independently rather than rushing a mechanism.

Local explanations: Spectroscopy and synthesis evidence.

Working definitions

Use full-headed arrows for electron pairs: start at a lone pair or bond and end at its acceptor. Keep ordinary carbon to four bond orders and conserve atoms and net charge. For compatible proton-transfer data, K≈10^(product-acid pKa−reactant-acid pKa). For amounts use n=m/M and 1 mol=1000 mmol; compare n/coefficient before predicting theoretical product. Apparent isolated yield=100×isolated/theoretical product mass. TLC (thin-layer chromatography) uses Rf=spot/front distances from the same origin. For simple first-order NMR, n adjacent equivalent H give n +1 lines while integration counts H area. Any further structure, constant or observation you need is given in the problem.

1. Constrain an unknown formula

Unknown V has C₄H₈O₂. Use DBE=(2C+2−H)/2 and draw one ester and one acid compatible with its value.

Hint

O is omitted in this simple C/H/O count.

Worked explanation

DBE=1. CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ and CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH both fit. The formula constrains composition but does not choose connectivity.

2. Read functional-group evidence

V has a complete simulated IR trace with strong carbonyl near 1740 cm⁻¹ and no broad O–H. Which of those two candidates is challenged, assuming adequate coverage?

Hint

A carboxylic acid predicts a broad OH envelope.

Worked explanation

Butanoic acid is challenged; the ester remains consistent. Absence evidence depends on detection and coverage, and one carbonyl feature does not uniquely prove the ester.

3. Join two proton environments

V has 1.25 ppm triplet 3H and 4.12 ppm quartet 2H. Use the simple n +1 rule to explain both linesets and propose a connected fragment.

Hint

Triplet sees 2H; quartet sees 3H.

Worked explanation

The pair fits O–CH₂–CH₃: CH₂ is split by 3H and methyl by 2H, with 2:3 integration. The downfield CH₂ shift helps place O; multiplicity alone would not locate that atom.

4. Place a third environment

A remaining 2.05 ppm singlet integrates 3H. Assign it in CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ and explain why the distant O–CH₂ does not give ordinary vicinal splitting.

Hint

The acetyl methyl’s adjacent carbon is the carbonyl carbon.

Worked explanation

CH₃–C(=O) gives the singlet because adjacent carbonyl C has no H. The O–CH₂ protons are not vicinal neighbours of that methyl in this simple model.

5. Use carbon evidence

V has simulated ¹³C signals 14,21,60,171 ppm. Assign them to the proposed ester and explain whether peak heights count carbons.

Hint

Separate ester carbonyl, O-bound CH₂ and two methyls.

Worked explanation

171=C=O,60=O–CH₂,14=terminal ethyl CH₃,21=acetyl CH₃. Routine carbon intensities are not reliable quantitative carbon counts; environment count and formula must be checked together.

6. Test a competing structure

Compare V with CH₃COCH(OH)CH₃, also C₄H₈O₂. Give two differences predicted by that hydroxyketone.

Hint

Its oxygen-bearing carbon is CH rather than CH₂.

Worked explanation

It predicts an OH-bearing 1H environment and a methyl beside one H, commonly a doublet, unlike V’s 2H quartet/3H triplet pair. It also predicts OH functionality in suitable conditions.

7. Calculate reagent amounts

A supplied 1:1 reaction uses 0.750 g reagent A at 150.0 g/mol and 0.900 g reagent B at 100.0 g/mol, giving one product P per reaction extent. Calculate mmol and limiting reagent.

Hint

Use n=m/M and compare n/coefficient.

Worked explanation

A=0.00500 mol=5.00 mmol; B=0.00900 mol=9.00 mmol. A limits the desired reaction to 5.00 mmol product.

8. Predict and compare product mass

For that reaction, P has M=200.0 g/mol and isolate mass 0.720 g. Calculate theoretical mass and apparent isolated yield and state the purity assumption.

Hint

Theoretical product amount is 5.00 mmol from the supplied limiter.

Worked explanation

Theoretical mass=0.00500×200.0=1.000 g. Apparent yield=72.0%. This assumes weighed isolate is product; it does not establish purity or actual conversion.

9. Interpret TLC references

Under one condition front distance is 6.0 cm, A reference 1.8 cm, P reference 3.6 cm and crude spots at both. Calculate Rf and state the strongest modest conclusion.

Hint

All distances start at the same origin.

Worked explanation

Rf A=0.30 and P=0.60. The crude has at least two detected components with matching retention to the references under those conditions, without unique identity proof or quantitative conversion.

10. Recognise unresolved mixtures

An isolate shows one TLC spot atRf 0.60 but extra major NMR signals. Explain why the observations can coexist and propose a targeted next check.

Hint

Different compounds may co-migrate or evade the detection method.

Worked explanation

The single retention feature can hide a mixture. Compare full spectral assignments or use a suitable second separation condition that tests the extra component. Do not overrule NMR using the spot count.

11. Check independent mass inputs

A report recomputes theoretical mass from isolated mass rather than starting reagents. Explain why this makes the yield circular and give the correct calculation chain.

Hint

The denominator must be predicted independently of recovered mass.

Worked explanation

Starting masses→mol→limiting extent→theoretical P mol→theoretical P mass. Independently measured isolated mass enters only the yield numerator. Reusing it to define the theoretical denominator destroys the intended comparison.

12. Write one supported conclusion

For V, all spectral observations are simulated and fit the ester candidate; the separate reaction data give 72% apparent yield. Can you claim these spectra identify that reaction’s real isolate?

Hint

Do not merge unrelated teaching datasets into one experimental story.

Worked explanation

No. The V model supports a candidate assignment for its supplied data, and the amount exercise gives 72% under its product-mass assumption. Real-isolate identity requires appropriately measured and linked sample evidence, not an unrelated simulated trace.

Review your reasoning

Choose two corrected errors: one in a drawing/calculation and one in an interpretation. Explain why the corrected answer follows from the supplied inputs. Keep the first attempt so you can see the change in your reasoning.

Explore

Question: is an unmeasured spectral region the same as an observed absence, and can partial peak information justify a complete assignment?

Allow 9 minutes: predict for 2, mask and restore evidence for 4, then audit two contrasting claims for 3. The molecule does not change when observations are hidden; all data are fixed simulated U1 teaching observations.

Enable JavaScript to change available evidence and inspect which claims remain justified.

Predict

The supplied formula is C₄H₈O₂. If an IR measurement covers only the carbonyl window, can you claim that a broad O–H feature was absent? If NMR supplies only relative integrated areas 3:3:2, can you place an O–CH₂ group without shifts or splitting?

In the simple NMR model, integration gives H-area ratios while chemical shifts and multiplicities provide different clues. Missing observations are not negative observations.

Explore

  1. Begin with the missing-region preset and audit The O–H absence is observed.
  2. Restore full IR coverage. Identify what new observation now supports or challenges the acid candidate.
  3. Change NMR from relative areas only to splitting only, then to full evidence. Compare Integrals account for eight H with O–CH₂–CH₃ is assigned.
  4. Try the purity and unique-structure claims, even with complete evidence.

Explain and check

A carbonyl-only window cannot establish O–H absence; the grey area explicitly marks unmeasured data. Reliable relative areas can normalise to 3:3:2 and account for eight H using the formula, but they do not by themselves locate those H in the molecule. A 2H quartet near 4.12 ppm with the corresponding 3H triplet provides additional support for O–CH₂–CH₃ in the stated candidate comparison.

Complete U1 observations favour ethyl ethanoate over the supplied acid and hydroxyketone alternatives. They do not establish purity or exclude every conceivable molecular structure.

Model limits

The coverage choices stipulate which observations are available and whether O–H detection is adequate. They do not simulate instrumental noise or compute quantitative confidence probabilities. Use the local spectroscopy explanations to connect each observation to a structural prediction.
