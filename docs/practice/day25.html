Day 25: Exercises — Final synthesis and interpretation

Combine reaction amounts, stereochemical drawings and spectral observations into justified chemical conclusions.

Allow 150 minutes: 10 minutes for retrieval, 48 for exercises 1–6, 48 for 7–10, 34 for 11–12 and 10 to review your solutions. Drawing and comparison are included in that time. Finish remaining problems independently rather than rushing a mechanism.

Local explanations: Integrated chemistry, spectroscopy and practical reporting.

Working definitions

Use full-headed arrows for electron pairs: start at a lone pair or bond and end at its acceptor. Keep ordinary carbon to four bond orders and conserve atoms and net charge. For compatible proton-transfer data, K≈10^(product-acid pKa−reactant-acid pKa). For amounts use n=m/M and 1 mol=1000 mmol; compare n/coefficient before predicting theoretical product. Apparent isolated yield=100×isolated/theoretical product mass. TLC uses Rf=spot/front distances from the same origin. For simple first-order NMR, n adjacent equivalent H give n +1 lines while integration counts H area. Every additional structure, constant and observation needed is supplied in the individual problem; no unseen textbook table is assumed.

1. Read a new secondary alcohol

For CH₃CH(OH)CH₂COOH, identify the alcohol type, acid and possible tetrahedral stereocentre. List four groups at the OH-bearing carbon.

Hint

The changing centre is bonded to CH₃ and CH₂COOH, not directly to carbonyl carbon.

Worked explanation

It is a secondary alcohol and carboxylic acid with substituents OH,H,CH₃,CH₂COOH at the tetrahedral carbon. Those groups differ, so that carbon is a stereocentre.

2. Rank the new carbon branches

At that centre compare CH₂COOH with CH₃ using attached-atom lists C,H,H versus H,H,H. Give full priorities with O and H included.

Hint

Break the first C/C tie at the next attached atoms.

Worked explanation

CH₂COOH outranks CH₃ because C outranks H at the first difference. Full order is OH>CH₂COOH>CH₃>H. Priority follows atomic comparison, not a memorised lactic-acid label.

3. Transfer acid–base reasoning

Draw hydroxide removal of the COOH proton from CH₃CH(OH)CH₂COOH with both arrows. Does that elementary transfer invert the OH-bearing stereocentre?

Hint

Follow the acidic H and the original O–H bonding pair.

Worked explanation

The product is CH₃CH(OH)CH₂COO⁻+H₂O. Base pair→H and acid O–H pair→acid O. Bonds at the stereocentre are not rearranged by this step, so it does not itself invert.

4. Transfer oxidation reasoning

Oxidise the secondary alcohol of CH₃CH(OH)CH₂COOH to a ketone while retaining skeleton. Give product and identify whether the former centre remains stereogenic.

Hint

The OH-bearing CH becomes C=O.

Worked explanation

CH₃C(=O)CH₂COOH forms as the selected target. The former centre is trigonal planar and no longer the tetrahedral stereocentre. Conditions/selectivity still matter in an actual reaction.

5. Distinguish ester from amide

Compare CH₃C(=O)OCH₃ and CH₃C(=O)NHCH₃. Identify common electrophilic site and explain a structural reason their chemistry differs.

Hint

Both have an acyl carbon; only the amide has N-pair donation into it.

Worked explanation

Both initially accept a suitable nucleophile at carbonyl carbon. Amide resonance stabilisation and leaving-group behaviour differ from ester, so common carbonyl shape does not imply equal reactivity or conditions.

6. Repair an acid–amine shortcut

For CH₃COOH+CH₃CH₂NH₂, use pKa 4.8 for acid and 10.7 for ethylammonium. Predict the initial favoured pair and explain why the result is not an amide bond.

Hint

Compare acid strengths in the proton-transfer equation.

Worked explanation

CH₃COO⁻+CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺ are favoured, with K≈10^5.9. This transfers H, not an acyl group. Amide formation needs suitable activation/conditions and a valid addition–elimination pathway.

7. Resolve a formula-compatible unknown

U has C₄H₈O₂, C=O IR without broadOH, and proton peaks 1.25 t 3H,2.05 s 3H,4.12 q 2H. Assign the ester candidate and reject the acid with two independent clues.

Hint

Use the full ethyl/acetyl pattern, not just one triplet.

Worked explanation

CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ fits O–CH₂ quartet, ethyl methyl triplet and acetyl methyl singlet. Butanoic acid predicts acid OH and lacks O–CH₂ plus isolated acetyl methyl; terminal triplet alone could occur in either.

8. Keep model claims honest

The unknown spectra above are numerically simulated. A student uses the plot to claim their own unrelated isolate was measured and pure. Identify both unsupported parts of that claim.

Hint

Provenance and purity each need their own evidence.

Worked explanation

A generated teaching trace is not a measurement of the student’s isolate. Even an appropriate measured functional-group spectrum alone would not necessarily prove purity. Sample-linked measurements and suitable composition checks are needed.

9. Calculate a new limiting case

A1:1 reaction has A=0.00400 mol, B=0.00600 mol and product coefficient 1. Product M=125.0 g/mol. Calculate limiter and theoretical mass.

Hint

Compare n/coefficient, then convert product mol to g.

Worked explanation

A limits at 0.00400 mol product. Theoretical mass=0.00400×125.0=0.500 g. Excess B does not increase that stoichiometric maximum.

10. Interpret a high apparent yield

The previous theoretical mass is 0.500 g and isolate is 0.480 g. Calculate apparent yield. If NMR contains major extra signals, what should be investigated before stating pure product?

Hint

A plausible mass fraction does not establish the numerator’s composition.

Worked explanation

Apparent yield=96.0%, assuming product mass. Investigate residual solvent, another component, unreacted starting material or assignment error using appropriate full evidence; the problem does not identify a unique impurity.

11. Audit a TLC conclusion

A reference and isolate each have one spot atRf 0.50 under one condition. Explain the supported comparison and two reasons it does not uniquely prove identity/purity.

Hint

Ask what separation and detection could fail to distinguish.

Worked explanation

Their detected retention matches under those conditions. Co-migration can hide distinct compounds, and some components may not be detected. A single matching spot is useful comparison evidence rather than a unique molecular proof.

12. Write a final conclusion and next test

Combine the hypothetical reaction amounts,96% apparent yield and uncertain NMR from these exercises into a short claim with limitation and targeted next observation.

Hint

Include the quantity actually calculated and the evidence that challenges its interpretation.

Worked explanation

The mass calculation gives 96% apparent isolated yield under the product-mass assumption, but unexpected NMR makes purity/identity uncertain. A complete spectral assignment and suitable comparison or separation should test a specific remaining alternative before claiming successful pure-product isolation.

Review your reasoning

Choose two corrected errors: one in a drawing/calculation and one in an interpretation. Explain why the corrected answer follows from the supplied inputs. Keep the first attempt so you can see the change in your reasoning.

Explore

Question: which claims about amount, conversion, identity and purity follow from the supplied observations—and which remain unsupported?

Allow 10 minutes: predict for 2, change evidence for 5, then write a restrained conclusion for 3. All observations are hypothetical teaching scenarios; no actual isolate has been measured by this demo.

Enable JavaScript to vary isolated mass and evidence provenance and inspect the supported claims.

Predict

The hypothetical reaction is 1:1 with 10.00 mmol limiting reagent, one product per reaction extent, and product molar mass 180.16 g/mol. Calculate its theoretical product mass. Predict apparent isolated yield for 1.20 g of isolated material. Does that calculation alone provide conversion or purity?

The product is intentionally unnamed. The unassigned pattern comparison is schematic; it is neither an aspirin measurement nor the U1 spectrum from the other spectral demos.

Explore

  1. Vary isolated mass and keep the starting-reagent amount fixed. Notice which derived quantities change and which stay fixed.
  2. Try the Unrelated-spectrum trap. Why can an apparently matching pattern from another teaching sample not identify this isolate?
  3. Link the pattern to this isolate, then introduce an extra unexpected signal group. Audit identity and purity separately.
  4. Compare one versus two detected TLC features.
  5. Supply the optional quantitative same-batch assay: 2.00 mmol starting material remains from 10.00 mmol initially. Calculate conversion, then try the high-mass contradiction preset.

Explain and check

Theoretical mass is \(0.01000\times180.16=1.8016\) g. For 1.20 g isolated material, apparent isolated yield is about 66.6%, conditional on that mass representing product. It does not measure starting-material consumption.

The optional residual assay independently gives conversion \(100(10.00-2.00)/10.00=80.0\%\). An apparent product-mass yield above this same-batch conversion calls assumptions or records into question; retained solvent, impurities or incompatible records are possibilities to investigate.

A consistent, explicitly linked structural observation supports an identity claim; an unrelated trace does not. Contradictory signals require explanation. One TLC feature does not prove purity, and two resolved features support multiple detected components under the stated conditions.

Model limits

The conversion assay is stipulated quantitative and linked to the same batch. The qualitative pattern comparison supplies consistency/contradiction only; it does not measure purity percentages or generate actual molecular spectra. All plausible causes of a discrepancy are hypotheses, not diagnoses. Conclude with a supported claim, a limitation and a targeted next observation, as in integration exercises.
