Day 17: Exercises — Acyl chemistry

Compare acids, esters and amides by drawing their addition and leaving-group steps. Work through 1–10 for core work, 11–16 for transfer and 17–20 for deeper checks. Allow roughly 2–3 hours across the unit, taking longer for complete mechanisms. Attempt each problem before using its hint, then compare your revised drawing with the worked explanation.

Working refresher

An acyl carbon is carbonyl carbon in R–C(=O)–Y. An ester has Y=OR′ and an amide has Y=NR′R″. A nucleophile donates a pair to carbonyl carbon while the C=O pi pair moves to O, giving a four-single-bond tetrahedral species. A viable leaving-group step can then restore C=O. Draw proton transfers with explicit donors and acceptors. Formal-charge checks used here: ordinary O with two bonds is neutral, one bond/three pairs is −1, and three bond orders is +1. For compatible pKa estimates, proton-transfer K≈10^(product-acid pKa−reactant-acid pKa). A worked mechanism is shown in the lecture; every problem below supplies its own necessary species and constants.

1. Identify four oxygen-containing families

Draw CH₃COOH, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃, CH₃CONH₂ and CH₃CH₂OCH₂CH₃ with explicit C=O bonds. Classify each, circle each acyl carbon and explain which one lacks an acyl group.

Hint

An acyl group contains a carbonyl carbon attached to the substituent being compared.

Worked explanation

The structures are acid, ester, amide and ether. The first three share CH₃C(=O)–Y; the ether has no C=O and therefore no acyl carbon. Oxygen alone does not establish an ester.

2. Read a condensed formula carefully

Expand HCOOCH₃ and CH₃OCH₃. Count the bonds to the carbon next to the first oxygen. Which compound can undergo the elementary carbonyl-addition pattern?

Hint

In HCOOCH₃ the first carbon is H–C(=O)–, not H–C–O–O.

Worked explanation

HCOOCH₃ is methyl methanoate, H–C(=O)–O–CH₃, an ester. CH₃OCH₃ is an ether. Only the ester contains the required carbonyl carbon for that addition pattern.

3. Write the two addition arrows

For OH⁻ attacking CH₃C(=O)OCH₃, draw the hydroxide lone pair and both curved arrows. Show a valid intermediate with all charges and count carbon bond orders.

Hint

As the new C–O bond forms, the C=O pi pair must move.

Worked explanation

Draw OH pair→acyl C and C=O π→carbonyl O. The intermediate is CH₃C(O⁻)(OH)(OCH₃), with four single bonds at C and net −1. Incoming O is neutral OH; original carbonyl O is O⁻.

4. Track three oxygen identities

Label ester carbonyl O as A, ester single-bond O as B and incoming hydroxide O as C. Draw the tetrahedral intermediate and identify which oxygen still bears a methyl group.

Hint

Carbon–oxygen bonds to substituents do not disappear during the addition step.

Worked explanation

The intermediate is CH₃C(O-A⁻)(O-CH₃-B)(OH-C). B remains attached to methyl. A bears the negative charge and C retains its original H. A label follows an atom, not a position on the page.

5. Complete an elimination step

Starting from CH₃C(O⁻)(OH)(OCH₃), draw carbonyl re-formation and methoxide departure. Give the immediate acid and base products and their total charge.

Hint

One pair moves from O⁻ into C–O π; another moves from the C–OCH₃ bond to methoxy O.

Worked explanation

The immediate pair is CH₃COOH + CH₃O⁻. Total charge remains −1. Carbonyl is restored, and methyl stays bonded to its departing oxygen; the group is methoxide, not a free methyl carbanion.

6. Add the missing acid–base step

After collapse gives CH₃COOH and CH₃O⁻, use approximate compatible aqueous pKa values 4.8 for CH₃COOH and 15.5 for CH₃OH. Draw proton transfer, state the favoured pair and estimate K = 10^(product-acid pKa − reactant-acid pKa).

Hint

The weaker acid has the larger pKa.

Worked explanation

Methoxide takes the acid proton to give CH₃COO⁻ + CH₃OH. K≈10^10.7≈5×10¹⁰ toward that pair. Stopping at neutral acid plus alkoxide misses a strongly favoured final proton transfer.

7. Balance net base-promoted hydrolysis

Write the net equation for CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ with OH⁻. Include the protonation state present in a basic mixture and audit atoms and total charge.

Hint

Cancel intermediates, not final stoichiometric reagents.

Worked explanation

CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + CH₃CH₂OH. Both sides have C₄H₉O₃ and net −1. Hydroxide is consumed overall, unlike the regenerated OH⁻ in simple carbonyl hydration.

8. Separate hydrolysis from acid work-up

The basic reaction mixture contains sodium ethanoate. Write its reacting anion with hydronium and give products. Does work-up form a new carbon skeleton?

Hint

A spectator sodium ion need not enter the proton-transfer step.

Worked explanation

CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺ → CH₃COOH + H₂O. The acid carbon skeleton is retained. Work-up changes protonation state; it is not an unexplained deletion of the negative charge.

9. Reject five-bonded carbon

A student draws CH₃C(=O)(OH)OCH₃ as the intermediate after OH⁻ attack. Count bond orders at carbon and repair the structure.

Hint

A carbonyl double bond contributes two to the bond-order count.

Worked explanation

That carbon has C–CH₃, C=O, C–OH and C–OCH₃: total five bond orders. Replace C=O with C–O⁻ to give CH₃C(O⁻)(OH)(OCH₃). The paired electron movement both restores carbon valence and locates charge.

10. Compare ester and aldehyde

Compare OH⁻ addition to CH₃CHO and CH₃COOCH₃. Draw both tetrahedral species and explain why an ordinary aldehyde does not normally undergo substitution by expelling H⁻.

Hint

Compare the substituent in the potential leaving-group position.

Worked explanation

The species are CH₃CH(OH)O⁻ and CH₃C(OH)(O⁻)(OCH₃). Methoxy can leave through an appropriate pathway; hydride is an extremely poor leaving group in these simple reactions. Similar first addition steps do not force the same later chemistry.

11. Start acid-catalysed esterification

Use ethanoic acid, ethanol and hydronium. Draw carbonyl-oxygen protonation, then neutral ethanol attack with movement of the pi pair. Give the charge on each oxygen of CH₃C(OH)(OH)(OHEt).

Hint

The incoming ethanol oxygen gains a third bond; the protonated original carbonyl oxygen loses its pi bond.

Worked explanation

Protonation gives CH₃C(=OH⁺)OH plus water. Ethanol attack gives CH₃C(OH)(OH)(O⁺HEt). Original carbonyl O is now neutral OH, original acid OH is neutral and incoming oxygen is +1. Total charge is +1.

12. Make the incoming oxygen neutral

For CH₃C(OH)(OH)(O⁺HEt), draw water removing the extra incoming-oxygen proton. Count bonds at that O after transfer and identify the catalyst-containing species produced.

Hint

The O–H bonding pair stays on the oxygen losing H⁺.

Worked explanation

Water pair→incoming-oxygen H; O–H pair→incoming O. Products include CH₃C(OH)(OH)(OEt) and H₃O⁺. Incoming O has two bonds and is neutral. Explicit proton donors/acceptors preserve both atoms and charge.

13. Create and remove a leaving group

From the neutral tetrahedral species, protonate an OH to OH₂⁺, then draw loss of water with carbonyl re-formation. Why is drawing direct OH⁻ loss inappropriate for this representative acidic pathway?

Hint

Protonation changes the identity and stability of the departing group.

Worked explanation

An OH₂⁺ substituent can depart as neutral H₂O while the other OH oxygen reforms C=O, initially giving a protonated ester. Deprotonation restores neutral ester and acid catalyst. Direct OH⁻ loss skips the activation central to this acid pathway.

14. Distinguish catalyst from equilibrium change

For CH₃COOH + EtOH ⇌ CH₃COOEt + H₂O, predict the effect of removing water and separately the effect of adding an acid catalyst at fixed temperature.

Hint

One action changes composition; the other changes access to the reaction pathway.

Worked explanation

Removing water can shift the composition toward ester. A catalyst accelerates the forward and reverse approach without changing K for the same net reaction under fixed conditions. Faster equilibration does not imply a different equilibrium constant.

15. Check an oxygen label in the product

In the standard esterification pathway, use ethanol labelled at oxygen as Et–O*H. Draw the labelled ester and say which bond links the labelled O to the acid-derived fragment.

Hint

The alcohol oxygen forms the new bond to acyl carbon.

Worked explanation

The ester is CH₃C(=O)OEt. O is the single-bond ester oxygen, directly attached to acyl carbon and ethyl. This follows the specified mechanism; do not track atoms solely by whether they are drawn above or beside carbon.

16. Draw amide resonance correctly

Draw neutral CH₃CONH₂ and its C=N charge-separated contributor. Give all O/N charges, total charge and the correct connecting arrow type.

Hint

When N donates into C–N, the C=O pi pair moves onto O.

Worked explanation

CH₃C(=O)NH₂ ↔︎ CH₃C(O⁻)=NH₂⁺. Total charge stays zero. Use a resonance double-headed arrow, not equilibrium arrows: these describe one delocalised amide with fixed nuclei.

17. Explain restricted amide rotation

Compare CH₃CH₂NH₂ with CH₃CONH₂. Explain why their C–N bonds differ in rotational freedom and why their N basicities should not be assumed equal.

Hint

Connect the real bond to its resonance contributors rather than declaring the C–N bond literally always double.

Worked explanation

Amide donation gives partial C–N double-bond character, restricting rotation and delocalising the N pair into C=O. A simple alkylamine lacks that same neighbouring carbonyl conjugation. The different electron availability changes protonation behaviour.

18. Predict acid plus amine

For CH₃COOH + CH₃NH₂ in water, use pKa 4.8 for the acid and 10.6 for methylammonium. Give the initially favoured acid–base products and explain why this is not already amide synthesis.

Hint

Compare the reactant and product acids.

Worked explanation

CH₃COO⁻ + CH₃NH₃⁺ is favoured, with estimated K≈10^5.8. This is proton transfer. Forming CH₃CONHCH₃ would require suitable activation/conditions and a productive acyl-substitution pathway; the salt alone is not an amide.

19. Read two biological linkages

A lipid fragment is R–C(=O)–O–glycerol and a peptide fragment is R–C(=O)–NH–R′. Identify each linkage, locate the electrophilic carbon and explain why their rates of hydrolysis need not match.

Hint

The carbonyl carbon is common; the substituent and stabilisation differ.

Worked explanation

The first is ester and the second amide. Nucleophilic acyl attack begins at carbonyl carbon. Amide resonance stabilisation, leaving-group behaviour, activation and reaction conditions differ, so a common motif does not imply equal reactivity.

20. Audit an incomplete mechanism

A proposed ester hydrolysis drawing adds OH⁻, loses the ethoxy group, writes neutral acid as final product in base and omits every charge. List three independent repairs and explain one experimental claim this mechanism alone cannot establish.

Hint

Check valence, proton transfers and the difference between a model and observations.

Worked explanation

Move the C=O pi pair during addition; show O⁻ in the intermediate and ethoxide on collapse; include acid–base transfer giving carboxylate and ethanol in base. The mechanism alone cannot establish isolated yield, purity or the measured rate; those require observations.

Consolidate your work

Choose one corrected error and explain what changed in your reasoning. Then complete the self-check. The reading guide lists the matching book chapters.

Explore

Choose electron-pair steps for ester hydrolysis and follow the acid–base trap. Allow 7–10 minutes: predict first, change one input at a time and explain the result.

Explore the chemistry

Prediction, exploration and explanation

Predict (1 minute). For methyl ethanoate plus hydroxide in the supplied basic model, predict the first electron-pair operation. Which group at acyl carbon could leave after a tetrahedral intermediate forms?

Explore (4–5 minutes). Try addition, then inspect the tetrahedral intermediate. Test both plausible collapse directions: forward methoxide departure and reversal with hydroxide departure. Follow the forward route, then choose what happens to the transient acid–methoxide pair. Test a hydride or methyl leaving-group proposal and read why it fails.

Explain (2 minutes). Draw the accepted addition, collapse and acid–base steps. Use the supplied pKa values to explain why stopping with neutral acid plus methoxide misses the base-condition product. What additional operation would be needed to obtain neutral acid after the basic reaction?

Supplied model and its limits

The selected model is basic hydrolysis of CH₃C(=O)OCH₃. Addition makes a tetrahedral O⁻ intermediate; collapse can expel methoxide or reverse by expelling hydroxide. Forward collapse gives a transient acid–methoxide pair. Compatible illustrative pKa values are 4.8 for ethanoic acid and 15.5 for methanol, so the proton-transfer equilibrium constant is approximately 10^(15.5−4.8)≈5×10¹⁰ toward carboxylate + methanol. Acid work-up is outside the interactive sequence. No rates, real operating protocol or universal leaving-group ranking is inferred.
