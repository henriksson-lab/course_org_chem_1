Use structure to explain a property and an IR observation

Allow 75–90 minutes. This is chemistry practice rather than a new R lesson. Draw each molecule before comparing it. You need the functional groups from structures.

1. Separate formal charge from partial charge

Draw ethanol with its oxygen lone pairs. Label the C–O bond’s direction of polarisation and the partial charges at its ends. Does a partial negative oxygen have to carry a formal −1 charge? Compare neutral ethanol with ethoxide, CH₃CH₂O⁻, and explain what the superscript minus tells you in the latter case.

Hint

Bond polarity describes unequal electron sharing. Formal charge is calculated from a Lewis electron count and is written as an integer for each contributor.

Worked check

The C–O bond is polarised toward O, giving δ− at oxygen and δ+ at the attached carbon. Neutral ethanol has two bonds and two lone pairs at O, so its oxygen formal charge is zero. Ethoxide has one bond and three lone pairs at O and formal charge −1. Partial charge and formal charge describe different aspects of the electron distribution; they are not interchangeable labels.

2. Find hydrogen-bond partners

For ethanol, dimethyl ether, propanone and ethanoic acid, mark suitable donor and acceptor sites. Draw one hydrogen-bond contact between ethanol and water, and another between dimethyl ether and water. Which molecule must supply the hydrogen in the second drawing? Avoid adding a covalent bond when drawing the interaction.

Worked check

Ethanol has an O–H donor and an oxygen acceptor. Dimethyl ether and propanone have oxygen acceptors but no O–H/N–H donor. Ethanoic acid has an O–H donor and a carbonyl-oxygen acceptor; the protonated acid hydroxyl oxygen is not normally treated as a useful hydrogen-bond acceptor in this simple inventory. Water can donate H to an ether lone pair. Draw the intermolecular contact as a dashed line O–H···O, keeping covalent connectivity unchanged.

3. Compare boiling points without changing too many variables

Ethanol and dimethyl ether have the same formula, C₂H₆O, and the same molar mass. Their approximate normal boiling temperatures are 78 °C and −24 °C, respectively. Explain why this comparison is useful for discussing hydrogen bonding. What would make comparison of ethanol with a much larger hydrocarbon less controlled? Does the higher boiling point imply a stronger covalent C–O bond?

Worked check

Similar size and formula reduce the size/polarisability difference. Ethanol can donate and accept hydrogen bonds between its molecules; pure dimethyl ether lacks an OH donor. A much larger hydrocarbon may have substantial dispersion interactions, so molecular size becomes an important extra variable. Boiling normally separates intact molecules; the comparison concerns intermolecular attractions, not direct evidence about a covalent C–O bond energy.

4. Explain a solubility trend carefully

Compare ethanol with hexan-1-ol, CH₃(CH₂)₅OH. Identify what stays similar and what changes. Predict which is more compatible with water and justify the prediction using both polar and nonpolar regions. Would your explanation establish the exact solubility in g/L at an unspecified temperature?

Hint

Both contain one OH group. Count how much hydrocarbon is attached to it; do not treat the group as a switch that makes every possible molecule soluble.

Worked check

Ethanol is more water compatible. Both have one OH group, but hexan-1-ol has a larger nonpolar region relative to its hydrogen-bonding head. The comparison supports a qualitative trend, not an exact g/L value. Temperature, detailed molecular structure and interactions in the separated phases also matter.

5. Read a simulated IR spectrum

The illustration is a schematic teaching spectrum, not a measured sample. First name the axes and explain why absorption goes downward. A supplied unknown has a strong absorption near 1715 cm⁻¹ and no broad alcohol OH feature between about 3200 and 3600 cm⁻¹. Candidates are ethanol, propanone and dimethyl ether. Which candidate best fits this restricted set? What additional evidence would you want before declaring the sample pure propanone?

Worked check

The horizontal axis is wavenumber in cm⁻¹, decreasing to the right; the vertical axis is transmittance. Absorption removes transmitted light, producing a dip. Propanone fits the strong carbonyl and lack of OH; the other candidates lack a carbonyl. The conclusion is relative to the candidate list. It does not establish purity or rule out all other ketones. NMR, further spectral features and suitable purity evidence would strengthen the interpretation.

6. Check a misleading explanation

A student writes: “The IR spectrum has a peak, so the compound is polar and must dissolve in water.” Rewrite the statement as two defensible claims, one about what IR probes and one about what solubility depends on.

Worked check

A suitable IR absorption indicates a vibrational transition involving a change in dipole moment and may support a functional-group assignment. Solubility depends on the balance of solute/solvent and solute/solute interactions, the molecular structure, charge and conditions. A peak alone does not establish water solubility or a nonzero permanent molecular dipole.

Leave with

Annotated donor/acceptor drawings, a controlled boiling-point explanation and a spectral conclusion that names both supporting evidence and its limits. Complete the self-check before the acid–base unit. Optional reference: selected Solomons chapter 2 topics, as described in the reading guide.
