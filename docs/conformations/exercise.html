Day 6: Exercises — Conformations

Draw Newman projections and cyclohexane chairs, then explain how a rotation changes the view and energy. Review the lecture when you need a drawing convention. Core 1–12: about 60–80 minutes with drawings and checks. Transfer 13–20: an optional 35–50 minutes for consolidation or later revision. Choose a smaller core subset when a practical session also includes R.

Tools and worked start

A conformation changes by an allowed spatial motion such as single-bond rotation without changing connectivity. Newman front carbon is the central dot; rear carbon the circle. Staggered bond sets are offset 60°; eclipsed sets align. For butane methyl groups, anti=180° and gauche= ±60°. A chair flip exchanges axial/equatorial and preserves up/down. For supplied effective free energies, w=exp(−ΔG/RT), fraction=w/sum(w), R=8.314462618J/(molK), T=298.15 K; degeneracy is included. Worked start: weights 1 and 1 give fractions 0.5 and 0.5.

Draw first, explain the rule you used, and open the check only after an attempt. Ordinary drawings may use a structure editor or paper.

1. Define the viewing bond

Number CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ C1–C4 and look C2→C3. Identify front/rear C and the three groups on each.

Hint

Nearest carbon is front.

Worked check

C2 is front, attached to C1 methyl and two H; C3 is rear, attached to C4 methyl and two H. Both carbon sets must be drawn.

2. Draw anti-butane

Keep front CH₃ at 12 o’clock; place rear CH₃ at 6 o’clock and complete both H sets in a Newman projection. State methyl dihedral.

Hint

Each carbon has three projected bonds 120° apart.

Worked check

The methyl dihedral is 180°. Front H at 4/8 and rear H at 2/10 give a staggered arrangement. Anti names the methyl relationship.

3. Draw one gauche arrangement

From anti, rotate only the rear set 120° while front stays fixed. State the resulting methyl dihedral and whether eclipsed.

Hint

A120° change from 180° reaches 60° modulo a full turn.

Worked check

Rear methyl reaches 2 or 10 o’clock depending direction, giving±60° to front methyl. The structure remains staggered and is gauche.

4. Find both gauche minima

Draw the +60° and−60° methyl arrangements along C2→C3. Explain what stays identical.

Hint

Use rear methyl at 2 and 10 with front methyl at 12.

Worked check

Both preserve connectivity and are equivalent in energy in an achiral environment for ordinary butane. They are distinct conformations, not constitutional isomers.

5. Draw an eclipsed view

Draw ethane along C–C with front/rear C–H bonds aligned. How does it differ from a staggered view?

Hint

Compare a 0° and 60° bond-set offset.

Worked check

Eclipsed projected bonds align; staggered bonds are offset 60°. The atoms do not form extra bonds just because their projected positions overlap.

6. Count ethane energy minima

A qualitative full-turn ethane profile has eclipsed maxima every 120°. Locate the staggered minima between them.

Hint

Start with a maximum at 0°.

Worked check

Minima occur at 60°,180°,300°. The three are equivalent because the substituents are H. Numerical barriers cannot be extracted from an uncalibrated schematic.

7. Compare butane minima

For ordinary butane, anti is lower than gauche. Explain the methyl-group arrangement and why lower does not mean exclusive.

Hint

Compare larger-group separation, then remember thermal populations.

Worked check

Anti separates the methyl groups more favourably. Gauche remains a possible populated conformation. A finite energy difference does not make the higher state mathematically absent.

8. Separate conformer from constitutional isomer

Compare anti/gauche butane with butane/2-methylpropane. Identify which relationship changes bonded neighbours.

Hint

Trace carbon connectivity before reading the view.

Worked check

Anti/gauche retain one connected chain and differ by rotation. 2-methylpropane has a branched carbon framework, so it is a constitutional isomer of butane.

9. Read a cyclohexane sketch

Explain why a regular hexagon for cyclohexane does not prove a flat molecular geometry. Name a common low-energy conformation.

Hint

A skeletal drawing first establishes connectivity.

Worked check

The polygon gives a six-carbon ring. Cyclohexane usually adopts nonplanar chair conformations; the flat drawing does not encode all 3D positions.

10. Track axial-up through a flip

Draw/build methylcyclohexane with axial-up methyl. Perform a chair flip and label the same methyl both ways.

Hint

Axial/equatorial changes; up/down does not.

Worked check

It becomes equatorial-up. Follow the whole ring rather than reversing one drawn bond. The process changes conformation while preserving configuration.

11. Track axial-down through a flip

Repeat with an axial-down substituent. Does down become up?

Hint

Use the same rule with the other side of the ring.

Worked check

Axial-down becomes equatorial-down. A ring flip exchanges axial/equatorial without changing the side of the ring.

12. Preserve a cis relation

Two labelled substituents are both up before a chair flip. State their cis/trans relationship before and after.

Hint

Cis/trans describes sides, not axiality.

Worked check

They are cis before and remain cis after. Up/up remains up/up even though axial/equatorial occupancy changes.

Optional transfer and challenge

These extend the same concepts without assuming later reaction chemistry.

13. Preserve a trans relation

One substituent is up and one down. Can choosing a different chair make this pair cis?

Hint

Track each substituent’s side through the motion.

Worked check

No. They are trans and remain trans. A conformation change cannot silently change the up/down configuration.

14. Explain methylcyclohexane preference

Compare axial and equatorial methyl chairs. Name an unfavourable interaction reduced in the equatorial form.

Hint

Think of groups at the 1,3 positions relative to an axial substituent.

Worked check

Equatorial methyl avoids the unfavourable 1,3-diaxial interactions present for axial methyl. This explains a preference, not that the axial form is impossible.

15. Check Boltzmann-model units

Use ΔG=3500 J/mol, R=8.314462618J/(molK), T=298.15 K. Compute RT and explain why ΔG/(RT) can appear in exp().

Hint

Both numerator and denominator must have the same units.

Worked check

RT≈2478.96 J/mol; ΔG/(RT)≈1.412 is dimensionless. Mixing kJ/mol with J/(molK) without conversion would produce a wrong exponent.

16. Normalise a two-state model

Given weights wA=1 and wB=0.244, calculate fractions. Are these already percentages?

Hint

Divide each weight by their total 1.244.

Worked check

Fractions are about 0.804 and 0.196, or 80.4% and 19.6%. Relative weights need not sum to one; fractions do.

17. Test zero energy difference

In a two-state model with degeneracy included, ΔG(A)=ΔG(B)=0. What equilibrium fractions result?

Hint

Both exponentials equal one.

Worked check

The two weights are 1 and 1; normalising gives 0.5 and 0.5. The qualification about degeneracy matters when grouping multiple conformations into one state.

18. Shift the energy reference

Add 1000 J/mol to both supplied relative state energies. Explain whether normalised fractions change.

Hint

Both weights receive the same multiplicative factor.

Worked check

Fractions stay unchanged because exp(−1000/RT) multiplies every weight and cancels in their normalisation. Only the relative difference matters for the ratio.

19. Distinguish equilibrium from rate

Two systems have the same state free-energy difference but different interconversion barriers. Must they have identical rotation rates?

Hint

A state difference and an activation barrier are distinct quantities.

Worked check

No. The equilibrium preference can match while rates differ because barriers differ. An equilibrium population does not by itself determine a time scale.

20. Identify a model boundary

A supplied two-state free-energy table already includes degeneracy. Should you independently multiply one weight by two because you remember two gauche views?

Hint

Avoid counting the same state multiplicity twice.

Worked check

No. The supplied convention already accounts for that effect. Adding it again changes the intended model. Distinguish a physical conformer inventory from an explicitly supplied effective-state model.

R task: turn supplied free energies into a population

Question: in a deliberately simplified two-state model, how much of each conformer would be present at equilibrium? The file data/conformers.csv contains relative free energies, ΔG, in J/mol, for A and B: 0 and 3500. These values are supplied teaching values and already include any degeneracy; they are not measured energies for the qualitative butane curve above.

At temperature \(T=298.15\) K and \(R=8.314462618\) J/(mol K), use \(w_i=\exp[-\Delta G_i/(RT)]\) and \(p_i=w_i/\sum_jw_j\). The ratio in the exponent is dimensionless. A is the lower-free-energy state, so predict A will have more than half the population.

library(readr)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
conformers <- read_csv("data/conformers.csv", show_col_types = FALSE) |>
  mutate(weight = exp(-delta_G_J_mol / (8.314462618 * 298.15)),
         fraction = weight / sum(weight))
knitr::kable(conformers, digits = 4)
ggplot(conformers, aes(conformer, fraction)) +
  geom_col(fill = "#176b91") +
  labs(x = "Conformer", y = "Predicted equilibrium fraction") +
  theme_minimal()
sum(conformers$fraction)

Add the result to your Rmd report. Check the sum and the direction. As a transfer task, replace 3500 with 0 in a copied data object, predict the answer and recompute. Then set both input energies 1000 J/mol higher, keeping their difference unchanged. What happens to the normalised fractions?

Numerical and model check

At 298.15 K, A has fraction about 0.804 and B about 0.196 (rounding depends on constants). Equal free energies give equal fractions. Adding the same constant to both energies multiplies every weight by the same factor, which cancels on normalisation, leaving fractions unchanged. The calculation assumes the two states adequately describe the system at equilibrium. It does not predict the speed of rotation; an activation free energy would be needed for rates.

Model and limits

The projection has a fixed C2 → C3 viewing direction. Blue bonds begin on the front carbon; dark bonds on the rear carbon. The curve is a smooth illustrative interpolation through canonical butane arrangements in arbitrary units, not measured free energies or a model of rotation rate.
